The story — what this is actually about

The real hook, in your own words: "I have found the transcript really useful to distil YouTube transcripts for resume guidance." That is the story — not "I built a RAG app." A person with a genuine problem (what do I actually do to my resume?) found the answer buried across a dozen hour-long career videos, and built the thing that extracts it. Everything measured downstream exists to answer does this actually work, or does it just look like it works?

Act 1 · The itch

Expert advice lives in hours of video. You cannot search it, cannot compare two experts, and cannot cite it. Watching is the only interface, and it does not scale.

Act 2 · The build

Six answer paths over one corpus — single-hop, recursive, agentic, GraphRAG, HyDE, contextual — plus document review, so you can point it at your own resume and get feedback grounded in what the experts actually said.

Act 3 · The proof

Advice you cannot check is just vibes. So every answer carries a timestamped citation, and every answer path is measured on the same labelled questions by the same judge — including the three that lost, published rather than buried.

The line the whole case study hangs on: "Paste your resume and it tells you what to change — and every piece of advice is a citation back to the exact second of the video an actual practitioner said it. The corpus supplies the criteria; your document supplies what gets critiqued." That is the thing nothing else does. Everything else in this artifact exists to prove the advice is real rather than plausible.
Supporting beat, not the headline: "I implemented three techniques the field is excited about — HyDE, multi-query, and Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval. On my corpus, all three lost to plain hybrid search. Here is the measurement, and here is the mechanism." This is still the strongest credibility signal in the work — but it answers can I trust this engineer?, not why would I use this?. It earns its place in the middle of the story, not the opening.

The problem, the user, and the impact claim

Problem

The best practical guidance on a niche — resumes and ATS, property tax changes, AI coding workflows — is published as long-form video by practitioners, not as documentation. It is unsearchable, uncitable, and contradicts itself across creators. You cannot ask it a question, and you certainly cannot ask it about your document.

What it does about it

Indexes a corpus of transcripts, then answers questions over it with timestamped citations back to the exact moment in the exact video — so every claim is checkable. Paste a URL (your resume, your LinkedIn) and it reviews that document against the corpus: your document supplies what is critiqued, the transcripts supply the criteria, cited separately.

Impact claim — keep it honest. This is a personal-scale research instrument, not a product with users. The defensible claim is about engineering rigour and a working tool for its author, not adoption. Say "I use it to distil career guidance from 26 videos" — true and concrete — and never imply traction that does not exist. A reviewer who smells inflation discounts everything else.

Finding 1 — the cross-encoder helps a bi-encoder ranking and hurts a fused one

pair (differs only by reranking)recall@3recall@5recall@10MRRNDCG@10
semantic → +rerank+0.058+0.052+0.008+0.052+0.036

The mechanism is the story: a bi-encoder never sees query and chunk together, so a model that scores the pair jointly adds real signal. But RRF fusion has already reordered using a second retriever's opinion — and the cross-encoder discards that ensemble judgement for its own. This comparison did not exist until the sweep was extended with a semantic+rerank column; before that, the shipped configuration was not even being measured.

Adversarial refinement loop — build, score, rebuild

Not one shot. The recorder produces a take, a separate adversarial reviewer scores it out of 10 against a rubric, and anything under 8 goes back to the generator with specific fixes. Max 5 rounds. The loop keeps the best-scoring take, never simply the last one.

I proved the reviewer mechanism before planning around it. Using the sibling's committed product_tour.mp4: parsed its SRT (11 caption blocks), extracted one frame at each caption's midpoint via ffmpeg -ss (~150 KB each), and read the frame back as an image. Three things came out of that test, and they set the whole design:
  • On-screen text is legible at the recorded viewport — so "can a viewer read this?" is a real, checkable question, not a guess.
  • Spoken claims can be checked against pixels. The narration said "nine hundred a week today, twelve-sixty proposed"; the header read $900/wk → $1260/wk. Verified. This is the single highest-value check in the rubric — it is exactly what would have caught our own stale "26 videos, 546 chunks" line.
  • It caught a real defect in the sibling video: the caption bar sits at top:80% and overlaps the plan canvas mid-content. That is a production bug a human reviewer skims past and a frame-by-frame reviewer cannot.

How the reviewer actually sees a video

It does not stream video. It receives one keyframe per narrated scene, sampled at the midpoint of each SRT caption, plus the full SRT text with timings. Eleven scenes means eleven images — enough to judge composition, legibility and claim-accuracy, small enough to fit one context. Motion is the one thing this cannot see, which is why the deterministic pass below owns timing instead.

Deterministic checks run first (no LLM)

Cheap, objective failures should never cost a model call: every scene has a caption; no caption exceeds the bar's two-line budget; no gap over 2.5 s without narration; audio duration within 1 s of video; SRT block count equals scene count; no error/failed text visible in any frame. Any failure short-circuits to a rebuild without consuming a review round.

The rubric — six dimensions, weighted

dimensionweightthe adversarial question
Claim integrity×3Does every spoken number match what is on screen in that frame? Any unverifiable claim caps the whole score at 6.
Hook×2By 20 s, does a viewer know what this does for them? Slides S1–S2 have to earn their place.
Legibility×2Is every element the narration refers to actually readable, unobscured, and on screen when named?
Pacing×1Dead air, rushed cuts, or a caption gone before it can be read.
Arc×1Does it land Itch → Build → Proof, or is it a feature tour?
Production×1Spinners, empty states, dev artefacts, overlapping chrome, stale corpus counts.

Configuring the reviewer to be genuinely adversarial

A reviewer that wants to be helpful scores everything an 8. Four settings prevent that:

  • Prosecutorial prompt — "find the strongest reason a hiring engineer would dismiss this," not "review this video."
  • Evidence required — every criticism must cite a scene number and quote the frame or caption. Uncitable criticisms are dropped before scoring.
  • No memory of its own scores — each round reviews the take cold, with the previous round's fixes but not its grade, so scores cannot drift upward by anchoring.
  • Weighted mean, hard cap — one failed claim-integrity check caps the total at 6 regardless of the other five dimensions. A beautiful video that says something untrue fails.

Why the generator is a data file, not a prompt

Feedback has to change something reproducible. So the scene list lives in demo/script.json — shot, selector, narration line, hold duration, per scene — and the reviewer's must_fix[] is applied as a patch to that file. Re-recording is then deterministic and diffable: you can see exactly what round 3 changed versus round 2, and the final video is reproducible from a committed artefact rather than from a conversation.

Cost and honesty controls. Each round is one recording (~3 min wall clock, Deepgram TTS only re-synthesises changed lines since the cache is keyed on text) plus one review call with ~11 images. Five rounds is the ceiling, not the target — the loop exits the moment it clears 8. Every round's score and verdict is written to demo/artifacts/review_log.json and committed: if the final video shipped at 7.4 after five rounds, that number is in the repo. A refinement loop whose failures are invisible is just a slower one-shot.

Product roadmap — ranked by resume impact

What goes in the write-up's "what's next" section, and what actually gets built after this task.

1Deploy behind an auth gate very high2–4 days

A URL answers "is this real" in a way no repo can. Google OAuth as a gate, not accounts; demo-mode history so the shared git-tracked file is never written; hard daily spend cap. The case study gains a "try it live" link — which is why it ranks first after this task.

2Distilled expert agents high1–2 weeks

Cluster the corpus, distil each cluster into a cited knowledge card, route questions to the right expert. The only remaining idea that adds a different kind of signal — offline compilation, versioned artifacts, a router — plus a score-vs-corpus-size learning curve almost nobody produces. Spec is already written: .lavish/s08_expert-agents-plan.html.

3Trends over time medium-high3–5 days

The one you named, and it is closer than it looks: the GraphRAG temporal route already builds dated claim timelines from upload_date, and the recency-weighting design from s08 already exists on paper. "How did expert consensus on X shift over 18 months" is a genuinely product-shaped question that no competitor chatbot answers — and it is a strong demo.

4Corpus expansion + a second vertical medium2–3 days

26 videos is thin for the career expert (6 source videos). Doubling the corpus makes every finding sturdier and is the precondition for the expert-agents learning curve to say anything. Low glamour, high leverage — and mostly compute, not engineering.

5Deep research mode medium-low~1 week

Becoming commodity — every framework ships a tutorial, and the agentic ReAct and recursive paths it would extend already exist here. The only differentiating version is evaluating long-form research: citation precision, rubric-scored reports. Real work, modest delta. Backlog unless a target role names it.

6Multi-tenant SaaS lowweeks

User accounts, per-user corpora, billing. Weeks of undifferentiated plumbing that signals nothing about AI engineering. Explicitly not recommended — the case study gets a short "what production multi-tenancy would require" section instead. The analysis is the signal.
