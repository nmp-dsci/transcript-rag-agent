New channels, and the refresh problem underneath them

The corpus watches 132 YouTube channels and nothing else. You want the same pipeline pointed at free text sources — the practitioner blogs and repos from s53 — and you named the hard part yourself: the URLs need refreshing. That turns out to be two different problems needing two different mechanisms. Every number below was probed against the live web today, including four things that do not work. The four decisions at the bottom are answered and the pipeline is built: the seed set is indexed, the four transcript collections are byte-identical, and a forced re-visit of all eleven sources re-embeds nothing.

The decision, stated once

A channel stops meaning "a YouTube channel" and starts meaning a watched source of URLs. That is the whole generalisation. A channel is asked "what is here now", answers with candidate URLs, and remembers just enough to make the next ask cheap. Four poller kinds cover every free text source in s53 — rss, atom, sitemap, github_docs — plus url_list for hand-curated one-offs, which polls nothing by design.

"URLs need refreshing" is two problems, not one. (A) Discovery refresh — new things appear at a source you are already watching. (B) Content refresh — a URL you already stored has moved, died, or quietly changed underneath you. A is a feed poll with a conditional GET; B is a state machine over stored sources. Building only A gives you a corpus that silently rots; building only B gives you a corpus that never grows. Section 05 does both.

Half of this pipeline already exists. src/documents/fetch.py and extract.py already fetch an arbitrary URL under five safety bounds and cut it into heading-delimited sections. What is missing is in front of them (nothing in src/ parses a feed or a sitemap — zero lines) and behind them (an extracted document is never chunked or embedded). This plan is a front end and a back end around a middle that works.

Yes, it goes into Chroma — in its own collection, not the transcript one. The store you have today is the store this uses: .yt-agent/chroma, 383 MB, five collections. Web sources get two new ones, web_sources and web_chunks, and every existing collection is left byte-identical. That is what keeps the committed eval snapshots honest, and it is what makes s54's Postgres move a later backend swap rather than a prerequisite. Section 02 has the table.

YouTube stays manual, and nothing here touches it. Your call, and it simplifies the plan: there is no youtube_channel poller, no automatic transcript fetching, and no new way to spend a Supadata credit. Videos keep arriving exactly as they do today — you paste a video or a channel into the ingest form and the existing queue handles it. This pipeline only ever adds text sources, and it needs no API key of any kind to do it.

The pipeline, and where the two refreshes attach

Left to right is one pass over one source. The two dashed arcs are the only loops, and they are the whole subject of this plan: the amber one asks whether anything new appeared, the green one asks whether what you already stored is still true. Everything from fetch rightwards is code that runs today.

Which store, concretely

Counted in .yt-agent/chroma today. Two collections are added and none is altered — the whole point of the separation, because transcript_chunks is the collection the committed eval snapshots in evals/runs/ were measured against.

Chroma collectionToday After this workWhat it holds
raw_transcripts339 339, unchanged One row per YouTube video: segments, metadata, summary state.
transcript_chunks8,026 8,026, unchanged The retrieval corpus. Not written to by any of this. 12.3 MB of that 383 MB is the vectors themselves, at 384 dims.
transcript_summaries337 337, unchanged Routing summaries and their embeddings.
transcript_chunks_contextual546 546, unchanged The parallel contextual-retrieval index, kept for its ablation comparison.
web_sources new— ~28 from the seed set One row per URL: extracted sections, the feed guid as identity, the URL history, and the refresh state from section 05. The analogue of raw_transcripts.
web_chunks new— ~1,400 from the seed set Section-anchored article chunks. Queried alongside transcript_chunks by a union at query time, so cross-source retrieval is a read-side choice rather than a mixed index. Estimated from the probe: 49.5 chunks per article mean, so about 2.2 MB of new vectors.

Two deliberate omissions. There is no web_summaries collection at first — routing summaries cost an LLM call per source and earn their keep only once there are enough web sources to route between, so that waits. And nothing is written to .yt-agent/documents, the 64 KB JSON store the chat's paste-a-link review uses: that stays a one-shot scratch space for a single reviewed page, which is what it was built to be.

And one consequence worth saying out loud. Because Chroma keeps one embedding space per collection and the union happens above it, similarity scores from the two collections are not strictly comparable — the risk section 10 names. Fusing them properly is the job reciprocal-rank fusion already does in src/rag/fusion.py for the hybrid retriever, which is the machinery to reuse rather than a new problem.

The rule the probe produced. A connector must declare whether the feed carries the body, because five sources are cheaper and more faithful read from the feed than from the page — three of them only usable that way — while four are unusable from the feed alone. The test is mechanical: if an item body exceeds ~2,000 characters it is the article; below that it is a teaser and the page gets fetched. Nothing about that needs a per-site rule.

And the rule it did not produce. Three of twelve have no usable change signal at all (C-06 and C-14). Any design that assumes ETags leaves part of the register unrefreshable, which is why the content hash in section 05 is the floor rather than the optimisation.

05

The two refreshes, each with its own mechanism

the core of the ask

You asked for a plan "where urls may need refreshing". Here is the split, stated as two tables and a state machine, because conflating them is the mistake that makes a corpus quietly wrong.

A · Discovery refresh — did anything new appear?

KindHow "new" is detected Cost of a poll that finds nothingFailure mode it has
rss / atom Conditional GET with the stored If-None-Match / If-Modified-Since, then new guids. One 304, no body. Verified on 8 of 12. A feed that renumbers guids on republish re-ingests its whole archive. Cap items per poll.
sitemap <lastmod> newer than the stored one, filtered by an include regex. One 33–70 KB XML parse. No 304 on either sitemap probed. A generator that stamps every lastmod on deploy looks like "everything changed". Confirm against the content hash before re-fetching.
github_docs releases.atom ETag, or the raw file's ETag per pinned path. One 304 per watched path. A repo that never tags releases needs the per-file ETag path instead.
url_list Nothing. It is a hand-curated list and only changes when you edit it. Zero. None, which is exactly why the seed set should start here.

Cadence. Start every channel at 24 hours. Double the interval on each poll that finds nothing, to a 7-day ceiling; halve it on each poll that finds something, to a 6-hour floor. Back off exponentially on error and disable a channel after five consecutive failures rather than retrying it forever — the same discipline the Supadata key ring got last week, for the same reason. One host at a time, robots.txt honoured, and a poll run that hits a dead channel records the error and continues.

B · Content refresh — is what I stored still what is there?

StateTrigger What it costsWhat the app shows
live304, or 200 with an identical content_hash. Nothing. No extract, no chunk, no embed. The whole point of hashing at the source level.Last checked, quietly.
changed200 with a new content_hash. Re-extract and re-chunk, then embed only chunks whose own hash changed. A typo fix in paragraph one re-embeds one chunk of 94, not 94 of 94. "Updated 3 days ago", and the previous revision stays citable.
moved301 / 308 to a new URL. One fetch. Identity must not move with it: the source keeps its external_id from the feed guid and gains a URL history. Without that, document_id_for(url) mints a second document and you have a silent duplicate. The new URL, with the old one still resolving in stored citations.
gone404 / 410, twice, a week apart. Nothing is deleted. Chunks stay retrievable and are marked unverifiable, because an answer built on them is still a real answer — it just cannot be re-checked. A struck-through link plus an archive link, not a dead blue link.
blocked403, 429, or robots.txt now disallows the path. Nothing, and no retry storm. Backs off and stays put. Named as blocked, so it reads as a policy decision rather than a bug.
truncatedThe body hit the byte cap. Already flagged by FetchedPage.truncated today — carried onto the source so a half document is never cited as a whole one.A warning chip on the source.

Why the hash is the floor and the ETag is the bonus. C-06 offers no ETag and no Last-Modified; C-14 offers neither of those and no lastmod either. For those two, "has this changed" can only be answered by fetching and hashing. Design for that case and the other nine get faster for free; design for the ETag and two sources have no refresh at all.

Fix 1 · a Markdown branch

extract_markdown(page) splitting on ATX headings, chosen by content type (text/markdown) or a .md path. Fifteen lines, and it turns GitHub docs from unusable into the best-structured source on the register.

Fix 2 · an article mode

extract_document(page, mode="article") drops nav/header/ footer and a leading section titled Contents or Table of Contents. The default stays mode="resume", so the chat review feature this module was built for does not change at all.

Fix 3 · prefer the feed body

When a feed item carries more than ~2,000 characters, extract from it and never fetch the page. Saves a request on C-01, and on C-08 it is the difference between the essay and a subscribe button.

Not fixed · JavaScript

Nothing here renders JS, so a client-rendered page yields its shell. Four of twelve probed fine; the rest of the seed set is untested. The honest answer is a per-source word-count floor that fails the ingest loudly rather than storing a nav bar as an article.

Volume, though

One hamel.dev post is 94 chunks — four times the mean video (8,026 ÷ 339 = 23.7). Four articles came to ~198. Article count is a bad proxy for corpus growth; character count is the one to watch.

What did not go wrong

The 2 MB cap was never approached (largest body 377 KB), nothing was truncated, and the SSRF bounds did not need touching. The fetch layer is genuinely ready.

07

The contract: one module, one protocol, one honest diff

Three artefacts carry this design. The committed list of what we watch, the protocol every poller answers, and the one change to existing code — which is deliberately the smallest change that could work, because fetch.py is the module with the security bounds on it.

channels.yaml committed: which sources we watch is a decision, not derived state

The split mirrors what this repo already does with guides/: intent is committed and reviewable, runtime state is not. ETags, cursors, due times and failure counts go to .yt-agent/channels/state.json, which is gitignored — so a poll never dirties the tree, and a cleared cache costs one extra poll rather than a lost corpus.

src/channels/models.py a channel is a watched source of URLs, and nothing more

Note what the protocol does not have: no fetching, no chunking, no store. A poller answers "what is here now" and hands back candidates. That is what lets V1 ship and be tested with no store decision made, and it is what keeps five source kinds from becoming five pipelines.

src/documents/fetch.py the only change to existing code — per-call content types, plus a conditional GET

Two properties worth stating. The default is unchanged, so the chat's paste-a-link path keeps the exact policy it has — widening the module constant would have let a chat message fetch XML, which nobody asked for. And only the content-type bound takes a parameter: scheme, address re-validation, redirect count and the byte cap stay non-negotiable, because those are the four that make this not an SSRF proxy.

The surface this adds. CLI: channels list | add | poll [--dry-run] | refresh [--due] and index-web <url>. API: GET /api/channels, POST /api/channels/poll, and a third mode value — "feed" — on the existing POST /api/index/queue, so a feed job rides the same three-worker queue and the same SSE progress stream as a video does. Demo mode refuses all of it with the 403 it already gives every mutating route.

10

Risks, and what I have not verified

  • Substack's robots.txt blocks named AI crawlers, and that is a policy question for you, not a technical one. Read today: GPTBot, ClaudeBot, anthropic-ai, CCBot, Google-Extended and eight others get Disallow: /; User-agent: * disallows only /subscribe, /action/ and similar, leaving /p/… article paths permitted. A generically named corpus agent therefore falls under * and is allowed — but the site's intent about AI ingestion is legible in that file, and robots.txt is not terms of service. Decision 3 chose the conservative reading, and my own option text got the consequence wrong: that blanket Disallow: / covers /feed as well as the article paths, so the source is excluded entirely rather than read through its feed. The exclusion is one host's configuration and not the platform's — the three Substack-hosted blogs on the register (C-11 to C-13) carry no such rule and are fully usable. hamel.dev, eugeneyan.com, huggingface.co and anthropic.com are all Allow: /; github.com sets a Crawl-delay, which is a reason to pull raw file URLs rather than crawl the site.
  • Feeds fail intermittently, so a poll run must not trust one response. Probing this session turned up transient 404 and 500 answers from a host that returned a clean 200 with full content minutes later. Without retry-with-backoff — the discipline the Supadata key ring just got — a poll run will report a healthy channel as dead, and after five such runs the scheduler disables it.
  • Long-form articles will shift the corpus mix. 94 chunks from one hamel.dev post against a 23.7-chunk mean per video. Twenty such posts outweigh the entire AI Engineer channel's 14 videos. Embedding is local and CPU-pinned so the cost is minutes, not money — but retrieval balance is a real effect and it is why the separate collection in V3 matters more than it looks.
  • Not verified: whether a mixed embedding space ranks honestly. s54 raised it; the new evidence sharpens it. Transcript chunks are conversational fragments that need the context header to embed at all; article chunks are self-contained prose four times the size. Similarity scores across the two are not obviously comparable. Measure it on the seed set before making cross-source retrieval the default.
  • Now measured: six of twenty-seven s53 links are JavaScript shells, and the floor catches every one. All 27 were run through the real pipeline: 21 clear the 250-word floor, and the six that do not are exactly the client-rendered ones — the three Substack-hosted blogs at 97–101 words (usable via their feeds instead), a Hugging Face Space at 12, ZenML's LLMOps database UI at 61, and a Hugging Face login page at 28. No false positives. The residual risk is the opposite error: a page that renders enough server-side to pass the floor while still being mostly chrome, which no word count can catch.
  • Feed archives are backfills in disguise. eugeneyan 212 items, ZenML 323, HF 863, OpenAI 1,215. A first poll without a cap ingests years of content, most of it off-topic, and one of those feeds is 742 KB with no conditional GET. Cap items per poll and filter by topic before ingesting, not after.
  • Identity is the one thing that cannot be fixed later. If web sources are keyed on document_id_for(url) — a plain hash of the URL — then every moved post silently forks into two documents, and un-forking them afterwards is a data-repair job over the chunk store. The feed guid has to be the identity from the first ingest. This is the one item in this plan worth blocking on.
  • Time. V0–V2 are a comfortable day and touch nothing that can break retrieval. V3–V4 are the real work. If the schedule tightens, stop after V2: pollers and fixed extraction with nothing indexed are useful on their own and cost nothing to leave sitting.
