Distilling the corpus into a reviewer

You asked whether there is something better than feeding chunked transcripts to a coding agent, and whether deep research helps. Short answer: your instinct is right, the technique has a name, and the literature is unusually clear about it — but the win is not a better retriever. It is a different task shape. This plan names that shape, maps the five most advanced distillation techniques onto code you already have, places deep research precisely, and — because your current approach already works — proposes the experiment that would tell you whether any of it is actually an improvement. Step 1 is fixing the judge (§3) — everything downstream gets scored by it, and today it cannot see depth at all.

Relationship to s08_expert-agents-plan. s08 designed distilled expert cards plus a router, for the task “answer my question as this expert.” This plan takes the harder half of your ask — “review my work through their eyes” — which is not a retrieval task at all, and specifies the artifact and evaluation that s08's pipeline would produce. They compose: s08 is the build pipeline, s11 is what it should build and how you'd know it worked.

1. The reframe: you are not doing Q&A

Every retrieval design in this repo assumes a question arrives and supplies the entry point into the corpus. Review has no question. You hand it a resume and say “make this better.” There is nothing to embed.

Q&A — what the repo does today

question → embed → top-k chunks → answer

The user's question is the query. Retrieval is well-posed. RAGAS measures it well: faithfulness, answer relevancy, context precision all assume a question exists.

Critique — what you actually want

artifact → ?? → findings

No query exists. Worse, the most valuable findings are about what the artifact fails to do — and absences cannot be retrieved by similarity, because there is no text to match on.

The load-bearing insight: in review, the query is the rubric, not the user. You must generate the questions from the corpus before you can retrieve anything. That single move — making evaluation criteria an explicit intermediate artifact instead of leaving them implicit — is what the strongest recent work has in common, and it is the reason chunk-dumping works at all.

Which explains your own experience precisely. When you paste chunks into a coding agent, the agent induces the rubric on the fly from what it happens to read, then judges against it in the same breath. That works. It is also: re-derived from scratch every session, invisible (you cannot see which criteria it used), uncorrectable (you cannot tell it “that criterion is wrong” durably), unevaluable, and silently dependent on which chunks you happened to paste.

3. Step 1 — make the judge measure depth decided — 40/60 + hard cap (D0)

You are right that the current measure is too light, and the code says exactly why. The composite is an unweighted mean of three metrics that all measure the same family of thing:

src/evals/judge.py:407

composite = sum(scores.values()) / len(scores)  over ["faithfulness", "answer_relevancy", "context_precision"]

Faithfulness asks is every claim supported by the retrieved context. Answer relevancy asks is this on topic. Context precision asks did retrieval rank the right things first. All three are grounding and on-topic-ness. None of them asks whether the answer is any good.

The concrete failure this produces. An answer that faithfully restates a single retrieved chunk, stays on topic, and cites well scores near 1.0 — while being shallow, obvious, single-source and useless as advice. A deeper answer that synthesises four creators and names a tradeoff scores no higher, and can score lower if synthesis costs it a little faithfulness. The current composite is not neutral about depth; it mildly penalises it.

Judge v2 — grounding as a floor, depth as the score

Keep the RAGAS three, but demote them to a floor that catches fabrication, and put the weight on dimensions that measure critique quality. Bump RUBRIC_VERSION from "ragas-v1" to "depth-v2" — that field is already recorded on every judgment (src/evals/judge.py:43), so old runs stay interpretable rather than silently incomparable.

The cost, stated plainly: this invalidates every cached matrix cell. src/evals/matrix_cache.py fingerprints a cell on the judging configuration as well as the answering one — deliberately, so a changed judge can never return a stale score. Changing the composite therefore forces a full re-run of the 20 × 4 matrix, not just a re-score. That is a known, bounded cost you have paid before, but it should be a decision rather than a surprise. Budget it once and land all the dimensions in a single version bump rather than iterating the weights across three re-runs.
Why this genuinely is the right step 1, beyond being your call. The five depth dimensions above are a first draft of the critique scorers in §8 — insight_depth, specificity and evidence_breadth are asking, in Q&A form, most of what finding quality asks in review form. Building them now means the critique harness is an extension of a judge you already trust, not a second scoring system with its own unvalidated opinions. It also re-baselines the whole scoreboard before any distillation work claims an improvement against it.

4. The proposal: a two-speed system

The single structural decision is to split what currently happens in one pass into two systems running at different speeds. Distillation is expensive, rare, and worth doing well. Review is cheap, frequent, and must be reproducible. Today both happen inside one prompt, which is why neither can be inspected.

5. The five techniques, ranked for your case

Each card carries its own architecture diagram — what the technique actually does to your data, and where it plugs into what you already have.

1
Rubric induction — make the criteria an explicit artifact
highest payoff

Do not map artifact → review directly. Generate the evaluation criteria first, judge each one atomically against retrieved evidence, then consolidate. The reason this wins is decomposition: direct generation conflates deriving criteria with judging against them into a single high-variance problem. Split, both halves get tractable — and the criteria become inspectable and correctable, which is precisely what your chunk-dump approach cannot offer.

Three rubric sources, all of which map cleanly onto things you already have: general (corpus-wide themes — your GraphRAG global mode already produces these), domain (per topic pack: system design, app architecture, resume, job search), and artifact-specific (generated per document at review time, evidence-grounded).

2
RAPTOR — retrieve at the right altitude
strong fit

A review needs principles (“a resume bullet that buries the outcome reads as a job description”), not sentences. RAPTOR embeds chunks, clusters them (Gaussian mixture + BIC), summarises each cluster into a higher-level node, and recurses — producing a tree where you can retrieve from whichever level of abstraction the query needs.

You are already two-thirds there and it is worth being precise about the missing third. You have level 0 (chunks) and a level-1 abstraction (per-video summaries). What you do not have is clustering that ignores video boundaries — and that is the level review needs, because “what do these four creators collectively believe about resume bullets” is not any one video's summary. Your graph communities partially cover this via entities; RAPTOR covers it via semantics. They are complementary.

3
Skill packaging — the literal answer to “into something usable”
format, not method

COLLEAGUE.SKILL is the closest published match to what you described, down to the input type: it accepts chat logs, work documents, public research material and subtitles, and emits an installable skill package. Its two-track extraction is the useful idea — a capability track (durable work methods, expert heuristics, source-grounded mental models) kept separate from a behaviour track (expression and interaction patterns). For your purpose the capability track is the product; the behaviour track is a distraction you should deliberately skip.

It also gets the lifecycle right, which matters more than it sounds: versioned corrections driven by natural-language feedback (“he would not say that”), with rollback. That is the mechanism that makes an expert improvable instead of regenerated.

4
Conflict-preserving synthesis — treat disagreement as the product
differentiator

Four creators on resume design will contradict each other, and a distillation step that averages them produces bland consensus — the least useful possible output. The literature distinguishes complementary from conflicting evidence by asking whether one person could plausibly hold all the views at once; complementary evidence should be fused, conflicting evidence should be surfaced as disagreement rather than resolved.

For a reviewer that is a feature, not a caveat: “Expert A would cut this section entirely; Expert B treats it as the strongest signal in the document; the axis is whether you're optimising for recruiter screen or hiring-manager read.” That is more useful than either verdict alone, and no chunk-dump produces it reliably because contradictory chunks just make the model hedge.

Concrete enough to implement. One directory per topic, versioned in the repo, human-readable, and installable as a Claude Code skill — which is the format you are already getting value from, just made durable.

How a pack gets decided

Your question from review. Packs are declared in a manifest, membership is computed by routing, and both are visible in the app before any build spends a call:

  1. A pack is declared, not discovered. experts/packs.json holds one entry per pack: a name plus a topic description written as routing text (“resume writing, bullets, ATS, recruiter screens, LinkedIn profiles…”). Creating a pack = adding an entry. Nothing about pack identity is inferred by clustering — clustering decides content, never which packs exist.
  2. Membership is computed, not hand-picked. Each pack's topic description routes through the per-video summary index (§4) exactly like a question would, giving every video a per-pack score; videos above threshold join that pack's build input. The D5 exclusion list is applied here, so the property videos can never enter any build.
  3. The assignment is reviewable before a build runs. A membership table in the app (Experiments → Expert packs): rows = 35 videos, columns = the declared packs, cells = routing score, with unassigned and double-assigned videos flagged. A cell can be overridden by hand and the override persists in the manifest — routing proposes, you dispose.
  4. Thin packs are visible immediately. system-design and app-architecture will show ~2 and ~0 members today. That is the V4b ingestion signal doing its job in the UI, not a routing failure.

One rubric record

Every field here exists to make a claim auditable — evidence is what lets a finding cite a timestamp, and contested_by is what stops the pack pretending to a consensus that isn't there.

Why JSONL and not a prompt. Because a criterion is a row: it can be counted, filtered by topic, individually scored against a held-out expert review, deleted when wrong, and diffed across pack versions. A criterion buried in a system prompt can do none of those things. This is the entire difference between a distillation you can improve and one you regenerate.

7. Where deep research fits — precisely

Deep research agents run a planning loop: decompose the question, retrieve iteratively, evaluate what is still missing, retrieve again, critique the draft, synthesise. The canonical split — GPT-Researcher's planner / parallel executors / publisher — maps onto your repo almost embarrassingly well, because you already built the executors.

planner
Decompose “what does this corpus say about resume design?” into 20–40 sub-questions.
Seeded from GraphRAG global themes and RAPTOR cluster labels, so the decomposition is corpus-grounded rather than invented.
executors
Answer each sub-question across your 6 setups in parallel.
This is the multi-modal sweep pattern for free — single-hop, recursive, agentic, GraphRAG, HyDE and contextual each find different things, and you already have the harness that runs all six comparably.
gap critic
“What did we not ask? Which claim has one source? Which is unverified?”
Its output becomes round two. This is the step that separates deep research from a big fan-out, and the one most implementations skip.
publisher
Emit the pack — principles, rubrics with provenance, disagreements.
Not a report for a human to read once. A structured artifact the fast path consumes.
What deep research does not buy you. Coverage and gap-closure, yes — measurably. Correctness, no. A deep-research loop over a corpus of four confident YouTubers produces a thorough distillation of four confident YouTubers. The thing that buys correctness is §8, and it is the part that is genuinely hard.

8. The experiment that decides whether any of this is better

This section matters more than the rest, because you already have a working baseline and no current way to beat it on paper. Even judge v2 from §3 cannot settle it on its own — faithfulness and answer relevancy presuppose a question, and review has none. But the depth dimensions you build in §3 carry straight over, so this is an extension of that work rather than a second scoring system.

MetricWhat it asksWhy it's the right question
Finding recall Of the issues the source expert raises, how many did the reviewer independently surface? The direct operationalisation of “through their eyes.” This is the study COLLEAGUE.SKILL says nobody has run.
Finding precision Of the issues raised, how many are supported by the corpus rather than model prior? Guards the failure your video loop already caught twice: inventing specifics that no source supports.
Provenance validity For each cited timestamp — does the transcript at that timestamp actually say that? Cheap, deterministic, no judge needed. You have the raw transcripts.
Contested coverage On topics where experts disagree, does the review surface the disagreement or silently pick a side? The one metric where a distilled pack should beat chunk-dumping structurally, not marginally.

The held-out design

  1. Hold out a video in which an expert critiques something concrete — a resume teardown, an architecture review. Exclude it from the corpus entirely.
  2. Give the reviewer the same artifact that expert critiqued.
  3. Score overlap between its findings and the expert's actual findings.
  4. Run the baseline in the same harness — chunk-dump into a coding agent, same corpus, no pack.
The honest risk, stated up front: chunk-dumping might win. At 715 chunks across 35 videos your entire corpus is roughly within reach of a long context window, and the crossover where retrieval pipelines become clearly worth it is usually cited around the couple-thousand-page mark — you are two orders of magnitude below it. So the distillation cannot justify itself on cost or on context limits. It has to earn its place on inspectability, correctability, disagreement handling, and reuse. Those are real advantages, but they are the ones you must actually measure, and the held-out test above is designed so a negative result is legible rather than embarrassing.

Your pre-filter point sharpens the fight. The baseline to beat is not a naive dump of all 715 chunks — it is a summary-filtered dump: route over the 35 per-video summaries first and dump only the matched videos' chunks (§4). On this heterogeneous corpus that is a materially stronger baseline, and it is the fair one, because the routing layer exists either way.

9. Build order — vertical slices, each with a loop gate

Structured for an agent building in a loop, on the same harness shape as demo/loop.py: deterministic checks run first and are free; the LLM judge runs second and costs money; a failed gate sends the findings back to the generator with the failure attached, not to you. Two rules hold for every slice:

  • Vertical, not horizontal. Every slice cuts backend → data → API → frontend and ships something you can click. No slice is “the backend half” of a later slice; there is never a big integration step waiting at the end.
  • The app is the review surface. Each slice names the exact click path at :8021 where you review it, because “done” means you can see it in the UI, not that a script printed a number.
  • Hypothesis-first review. Each slice states up front the falsifiable claim the feature should add. Reviewing a slice = walking its click path and checking whether the hypothesis is visibly true. 10. Risks & open questions

  • The off-mission quarter — resolved, D5: the 5 property/tax videos are excluded from all pack builds via topic routing (§6 “How a pack gets decided”) and stay indexed for Q&A. The residual risk is a routing miss, which V4's purity check (>20% property members fails the cluster) exists to catch.
  • Two intended topics have no material — resolved, D1: all four packs stay in scope, so system-design and app-architecture get a deliberate ingestion slice (V4b, ≥5 videos each). The residual risk is source selection: those videos do not exist in the corpus pipeline yet, and picking them is editorial work the loop cannot do for you.
  • Expert authority is unmodelled. A rubric mined from a creator with 200 subscribers weighs the same as one from a practitioner with real hiring experience. Nothing in the current pipeline captures that. Open: do you want a manual credibility weight per source video?
  • Recency. Resume and job-search advice decays fast; system design advice does not. s08 already proposed recency-weighted distillation — that should carry into rubrics, per topic.
  • Rubric count is a real cost. ~54 criteria per artifact means ~54 judgment calls per review. Fine offline, noticeable in a live UI. Rubric selection before judging is what keeps the fast path fast.
  • Corpus depth is topic-dependent, not uniformly thin. Resume/job has 22 videos and 321 chunks — genuinely enough to distil something that is not just a paraphrase of one creator. Every other topic has 2–4. So "is the corpus big enough" has different answers per pack, and only the resume pack can be answered yes today.
