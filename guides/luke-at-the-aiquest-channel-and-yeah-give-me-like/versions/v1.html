Four steps, two T4s, and a memory bank

How to generate AI video end to end on free cloud GPUs — picking a MiniMax H3 distillation variant, running the dual-T4 notebook, diagnosing the failures that only show up at 480p, bolting long-term character memory onto an open backbone with StoryMem, and knowing the exact moment the free tier stops being enough. The short answer

Yes — stage one runs for free, and no, you will not stay there. The corpus contains exactly one working end-to-end path for MiniMax H3 on free hardware, and it is a Kaggle notebook on dual T4 GPUs, not Colab. Two cells: cell one installs dependencies and pulls roughly 40 GB of weights in about three minutes weights, cell two splits the diffusion transformer across both cards at 9.3 GB of VRAM each and hands you a web UI split. A five-second 480p clip came back in 182 seconds 182s. That is your base case, and it is enough to do real comparative work.

On Colab specifically, be precise about what the corpus does and does not say. The same creator keeps "all my updated notebooks for Collab and Kaggle" in one GitHub repo repo — but the MiniMax H3 Turbo notebook is launched by clicking the Kaggle badge, and every hardware number given assumes two T4s with the text encoder pinned in ~21.9 GB of system RAM RAM. Treat Colab as unverified for this pipeline and run the verified Kaggle path first; see Gaps.

"Trying to run a massive model on a free T4 GPU is brutal." AIQUEST

That line is the whole reason the four-step distillation LoRAs exist, and why the free tier works at all. Three moves follow from it.

Buy speed with distillation, not hardware

Four-step distillation LoRAs are what make a massive model tractable on free cards, and their authors claim they "match or beat the full base model" claim. Distillation is the standard move — train a small student on a large teacher's outputs why it works.

Pick the variant by failure mode

Benchmarks do not decide this. PDMD leads published leaderboards on visual quality, motion and prompt alignment PDMD, yet Fused Turbo won the squirrel-on-a-deck composition test outright and lost the hard-cut test badly. Choose per shot type, not per leaderboard.

Buy memory with a LoRA, not a bigger model

ByteDance's StoryMem is a LoRA over an existing backbone that "generates videos shot by shot while maintaining a compact memory bank" StoryMem — 0.7 B added parameters on a 14 B base 0.7B. Minute-long continuity is an adapter, not a new model.

The four variants, and what each one is actually for

Cell one of the notebook exposes a single variable where you type the active LoRA: PDMD, DMAD, fused, or official switch. That one word changes your failure profile more than any prompt edit will. Here is the side-by-side, from the creator's own battery of text-rendering, physics, dialogue and multi-shot tests.

PDMD — sharpness and prompt obedience

  • Top of the published leaderboards against H3's official Turbo LoRA and DMD, leading on visual quality, dynamic motion and semantic prompt alignment benchmark.
  • Won the hardest test in the set — a 10-second multi-character argument with a prompted hard camera cut: "strict prompt following, PDMD easily took the crown" verdict.
  • Renders individual strands of hair crisply even at 480p; colour profile reads slightly washed out and muted next to Fused tradeoff.
  • Technically runs in two steps instead of four, "if you don't mind a noticeable drop in quality" two-step. Use it for thumbnail-grade previews only, never finals.
  • Text-to-video and image-to-video only — no reference-to-video mode modes.

Default Start here. It won the two tests that matter most for narrative work: legible text and obeyed cuts.

DMAD — cinematic motion, softer image

  • ByteDance's four-step distillation, claiming a 79% preference win rate against rivals like DMD2 and RCM in their own published data claim.
  • Trades harsh digital sharpness for motion that "looks organic and fluid" — the cat-on-a-turntable test read natural rather than artificial motion.
  • Weakest on kinetic impact: in the kaiju-versus-skyscraper test "the building just sort of collapses on its own" with no sense of weight physics.
  • Hallucinated extra text across a fighter's back and put "AI Quest" on a waistband instead of a belt in the text-rendering test text.
  • Cleans up the most of any variant when you move from 480p to 720p, "leaning into a remarkably smooth and realistic human render" 720p.

Pick for Sci-fi and action where smooth cinematic motion beats micro-detail, and for any shot you will finish at 720p.

Fused Turbo — fastest, widest mode support, least predictable

  • The LoRA is "baked directly into the model transformer safe tensors file" rather than loaded on top, cutting overhead architecture.
  • Finishes a standard five-second render 10–15 seconds faster than PDMD or DMAD, which share nearly identical times speed.
  • The only variant supporting all three modes — text-to-video, image-to-video and reference-to-video, the last taking reference audio plus up to three reference images modes.
  • Surprise winner on unhyped text rendering and on colour vibrancy — it "completely steals the show" on the spatial-composition test colour.
  • Breaks on the two things narrative video needs most: it faked a prompted hard cut by letting "the virtual camera just drift awkwardly back and forth" cuts, and "still lagged behind visually" in two-person dialogue close-ups dialogue.

Pick for Single-shot establishing frames, reference-driven generation, and any pass where render throughput matters more than character fidelity.

Official 8-step reference-to-video — the baseline you should keep

  • Built into the same notebook alongside the three four-step options, selected by typing official included.
  • Twice the steps means it is your control condition. Every "match or beat the base model" claim in this space is vendor-published; the corpus contains no independent third-party validation of any of the three win rates. Run the official model on your own prompts before you believe any of them.
  • Selecting fused or official makes the UI reveal a reference-to-video tab for reference audio and up to three images UI.

Always Render one shot per variant including official before committing a project to any single LoRA.

One result held across all four: audio is not the weak link. "The actual spoken audio and ambient background generation on all three models sounded fantastic," even where faces fell apart in the same clip audio. Budget your debugging time on pixels, not on sound.

Stage one: the free dual-T4 run, start to finished clip

Five stages. Nothing here needs a credit card, local GPU, or command line — the environment is "stripped down into just two executable cells" two cells. Budget around ten minutes of setup before your first render starts.

1

Verify the account before you touch the notebook

This is the single step that silently blocks everyone. Kaggle gates GPU access behind identity verification, and the failure is not an error message — it is a greyed-out dropdown.

  • Verify your Kaggle account with a phone number in account settings first. Skip it and "the GPU options will be grayed out and the notebook won't initialize" gate.
  • Set the accelerator to the T4 × 2 option, not a single T4 accelerator. The model is split across both cards; this is not optional.
  • Launch from the creator's GitHub repo by clicking the Kaggle badge next to the MiniMax H3 Turbo notebook — it "will load the workspace directly into your account" launch.
2

Choose the LoRA in cell one, then run all

The variant is a string literal, not a UI toggle, so it is decided before the 40 GB download rather than after. Pick deliberately; changing your mind means re-running cell one.

  • Default is PDMD. Backspace it and type DMAD, fused, or official to switch switch.
  • Cell one connects to the instance, installs dependencies and pulls ~40 GB of weights, "finishing the entire download in about 3 minutes" 3 min.
  • Cell two initialises the engine and takes "roughly 3 minutes" before it emits your UI links init.
3

Read the load logs — they are your capacity budget

These three numbers tell you exactly how much headroom you have, and they are why the free tier works at all. Memorise them; they decide every pivot decision later.

  • The script "automatically splits the massive diffusion transformer evenly across both T4 GPUs," loading exactly 9.3 GB onto each card split.
  • Auxiliary components — notably the text encoder — are pinned into system RAM, not VRAM offload.
  • Steady state: ~21.9 GB system memory, just over 10 GB per card steady state. Roughly 5 GB of VRAM headroom per card is all you have for resolution and duration increases.
4

Take the Cloudflare link, never the Gradio one

Cell two emits two public URLs and they are not equivalent. Choosing wrong costs you a mid-render session, and renders are measured in minutes.

  • You get both a Gradio link and a Cloudflare tunnel URL. "Grad links have a habit of timing out or crashing midsession" — use Cloudflare tunnel.
  • The UI shows your selected LoRA name at the top — use it to confirm cell one actually took your edit confirm.
  • The interface splits prompt input into dedicated fields for spoken lines, speech language, sound-effect prompts and background-music prompts. You can dump it all into the main box, but "splitting them out gives you granular creative control" fields.
5

Prototype at 480p, finish at 540p or 720p

Resolution is the main dial you have and it is a two-pass workflow, not a single choice. Everything you learn about framing at 480p transfers; everything you judge about fidelity at 480p does not.

  • "480p is your best friend for rapid prototyping," finishing a five-second clip in under three minutes; 540p is the middle ground, 720p maximum fidelity at longer waits resolution.
  • A measured five-second 480p run completed in 182 seconds, with full generation metadata saved below the video player 182s. Keep that metadata — it is your only reproducibility record.
  • Duration goes up to 15 seconds, with aspect-ratio auto-match and an image-to-video tab that takes first and last frames controls.
  • A style-LoRA picker ships preconfigured options (realism, people, camera motion, 360 orbit, weapon combat, spatial physics); you can "stack up to two styles at once" or paste a custom Hugging Face repo link styles.
  • Both PDMD and DMAD "hit their peak performance at 720p" peak, so re-render your final cuts at 540p or 720p to "clear up the artifacts" finals.

Delete on sight

Most 480p "bad model" verdicts are framing bugs. The corpus contains one controlled experiment that proves it: the same jungle prompt with hikers framed wide produced "noticeable facial morphing, glitching, and weird artifacts" because "480p just doesn't provide enough pixel density for distant facial features" root cause. Forcing the camera into a close-up at the same 480p made "the facial distortion disappear because the model has enough pixel real estate to resolve the eyes, mouth, and skin texture properly" fix. Framing is a resolution decision.

Long-term and short-term memory, concretely

This is the part of your plan that has a real answer in the corpus, and it is more specific than "give the model memory." Open video models have a duration ceiling: most "can barely generate 5 to 10 seconds of footage," and when they chain scenes you get "different faces, different outfits, completely broken continuity" the problem. Even Sora 2 "has a hard 15-second limit. You can't generate anything longer than that in one go" ceiling. StoryMem's claimed result against that baseline: 28.7% better consistency than Sora 2 and 9.4% over prior state-of-the-art HoloCine benchmark, over stories of 20, 43, 45, 46 and 58 seconds with 10–15+ distinct scenes.

Long-term memory: the memory bank between shots

The architectural choice is the lesson. Rather than train a giant model on long footage — an approach that "require[s] huge computational costs and often degrade[s] quality" rejected path — StoryMem keeps the single-shot model and adds state outside it.

  • It is "a Laura that works with existing video models," not a new model — so your backbone choice stays open adapter.
  • The bank "stores key frames from previously generated shots, characters, scenes, stylistic cues" — a compact visual-context store, not a text summary contents.
  • Memory enters the diffusion model via latent concatenation plus a negative RoPE shift, which "tells the model that these memory frames are from the past, not part of the current sequence" mechanism. That positional tag is the whole trick: without it, past frames read as present ones.
  • What goes into the bank is filtered twice: CLIP features for "semantic key frame selection to pick the most meaningful frames," and HPSv3 for "aesthetic filtering to make sure they're not storing blurry or lowquality frames" filtering. A memory bank is only as good as its admission policy.
  • Training cost stayed small: LoRA fine-tuning on "just 400,000 5-second videos," adding 0.7 B parameters to a 14 B Wan 2.2 base training. Short clips taught long-horizon memory.

Short-term memory: first/last frames and reference conditioning

Within and across adjacent shots, the corpus gives you three conditioning handles you already have in the H3 notebook — use these before reaching for StoryMem at all.

  • The image-to-video tab accepts first and last frames endpoints. Chaining shot N's last frame into shot N+1's first frame is the cheapest continuity you can buy.
  • Fused Turbo and the official model expose reference-to-video: reference audio plus "up to three reference images to steer your generation" reference — a three-slot short-term identity anchor.
  • StoryMem's own memory-to-video mode starts from an explicit initial memory: a 43-second lovers story began from "a male character and a female character with a garden background" seeding. You seed the bank; you don't hope it forms.
  • Environment consistency comes free with character consistency: in the cafe story "the cafe environment stays identical" across cuts for the full 20 seconds sets.
  • Set expectations honestly. Consistency is "quite good. Not absolutely perfect, but very solid" honest, and glitches do occur — one scene had a character "suddenly replace someone who was already standing" glitch.

What StoryMem costs you to run

  • Three weight sets on Hugging Face: text-to-video with Wan 2.2 14B, image-to-video, and memory-to-video — plus the backbone weights for Wan 2.2 14B itself weights.
  • Unquantised, "you can't run the full model unless you have serious VRAM" VRAM wall.
  • With an FP8 or FP4 quantised backbone, "you should be able to run this whole thing within 16 GB of VRAM" 16GB. That is the number to design your hardware around.
  • Why quantisation buys that much: dropping 32-bit weights to 8-bit means "75% of your memory is expected to be saved," applied after training purely to cut inference cost quantisation.
  • Blocker 16 GB in one card is not what the free H3 notebook gives you — it gives 9.3 GB on each of two cards with the model sharded across them sharded. The corpus never demonstrates StoryMem on a free dual-T4 notebook, and never shows a dual-GPU split for it. This is the hard boundary of your base case.

When to leave the free notebook — and where to go

Four triggers, each with a destination the corpus actually supports. None of them arrives on day one: do stage one first, learn your prompts and framing at 480p, and only then spend money.

Trigger: you need StoryMem's minute-long memory

  • Target one card with ≥16 GB VRAM plus an FP8/FP4 quantised Wan 2.2 14B backbone spec.
  • Stay on Nvidia. "Nvidia GPUs have access to CUDA" and it is the path of least resistance; AMD's ROCm is a viable but less mature middle ground CUDA.
  • Do not buy a Mac for this. "What I would definitely avoid is to fine-tune on Apple silicon" — missing ports, much slower than comparable Nvidia Apple. The corpus disagrees with itself here: Raschka trains happily on a MacBook Air CPU in ~15 minutes for a tiny 355M text model counterpoint — a different scale of problem entirely.

Trigger: you want your own look, as a style LoRA

  • Exhaust the cheaper options first. The decision order is: better prompt, then RAG/context, then an agentic loop, and only fine-tune if all of those fail order. For video, your "better prompt" rung includes the built-in style picker and stacking two styles.
  • LoRA trains roughly "half to 2% of the parameters" share by injecting "small trainable matrices into specific layers" while the base stays frozen mechanism.
  • Starting hyperparameters from the corpus: rank 16 — "16 is a solid sweet spot. I always use it" rank — with alpha equal to rank alpha, and a fixed seed for reproducibility seed.
  • Wall-clock reference point: LoRA fine-tuning a medium model took "2 to 3 hours on a 5090" time. Note these figures are all from text-model fine-tunes; the corpus gives no video-LoRA training benchmarks.
  • "Always always inspect your data before training. Garbage in garbage out" data — and dedupe near-duplicates before you start dedupe.

Trigger: you need unattended, scheduled batch rendering

  • A notebook is an interactive session, not a job runner. The one scheduled-automation example in the corpus carries the same caveat — automate the run on a clock, but the host machine has to stay on schedule.
  • The mental model is unglamorous: "deploying is nothing more than pushing all that code to some kind of like cloud server," setting env vars, and running the same startup command you ran locally deploy.
  • Containerise. "The really industry standard way of doing this is first of all to use docker" Docker; Railway for a first deploy, with its own MCP server for agent-managed deployment Railway.
  • For steady-state cost, a bare-metal provider beats hyperscalers at this scale — one team has run Hetzner for three years Hetzner and calls it "literally 10 times cheaper" than Azure/AWS for their workload 10x.
  • Expect the memory crunch to move, not vanish: a heavy document-processing library on a small cloud instance produced exactly the failure you'd predict — "we were running out of memory" — and the fix was a lighter library, not a bigger box OOM.

Trigger: clips need to become a channel

  • Pick the niche on revenue data, not taste: YouTube RPM ranges from "finance, that's $15 to $50 per 1,000 views" down to $3–8 for entertainment RPM — a 10× payout spread for identical effort.
  • Script to four rules: a hook in the first 30–60 seconds, an early open loop, visual tags on every scene for the editor, and "a soft CTA at the end so no smash that like energy" script. The visual tags are what turn a script into a shot list for H3.
  • H3 generates its own spoken audio, so voice cloning is optional — but if you want a consistent host voice, instant cloning "requires you to record a 10-second audio clip for minimum," with at least four clips recommended voice.
  • Watch advertised-versus-real tool pricing. An AI-avatar tool advertises from $15/month but realistic use needs "something like the $100 a month plan" for enough API credits pricing; most other tools' free tiers cover "80% of what you need anyway" free tiers.
  • Cadence and patience: "post five to six times a week," with results typically visible after two to three months cadence. At 182 seconds per five-second 480p clip, do that arithmetic before you commit.
  • For assembly and post, ffmpeg is the workhorse — one workflow generates start/end frames, runs them through a video model, then "break[s] out the video into individual frames using ffmpeg" for frame-level control ffmpeg.

How to actually tell which variant is better

Every number in the Variants section above is vendor-published. The creator's own method is the one worth copying: he "put together a dedicated comparison web page playing back my own pre-generated clips so we can watch all three four-step models side by side" harness, on his own prompts, "not cherrypicked marketing clips" discipline. That is a human eval harness. The eval videos in this corpus tell you how to make it rigorous.

  • Start in a spreadsheet, not a platform. "Spreadsheets are the ultimate sort of product to use for evaluating LLMs" before any automated judge exists spreadsheet.
  • Write the rubric before you grade. Define explicit criteria and "define what's good or bad for any of these" — for video, that means naming your axes: distant-face integrity, text legibility, cut obedience, motion weight, colour rubric.
  • Size the prompt set small at first: "somewhere between you know five at least usually it's around 10" golden examples, and only push past 100 when you're chasing production confidence size. At three minutes a render, ten prompts × four variants is already a two-hour batch.
  • Grade it yourself. Outsourcing human eval entirely "never [proved] useful" — the person who owns the product has to be in the sheet ownership. The strongest practitioner habit in the corpus is a principal engineer who "spends three hours per day looking at traces and data" habit.
  • Repeat each prompt. For probabilistic long-horizon generation where one bad step derails the whole run, "people usually run it three to five times" to get a confidence interval repeats. A single clip is an anecdote, not a result.
  • Take notes while grading, then compress them: write short notes next to the bad outputs and "use the LM to summarize all my notes" into the top few things to fix compress.
  • Ship something early. "Ship early and shipped often," even to a tiny cohort — production usage is where you discover what you actually needed to test for ship.
  • Calibrate your own expectations with the generic curve: a first build "typically gets to 70 or 80% accuracy. After iteration, you can usually get to 90%" curve. Your first batch of clips will not be your last.
  • Keep the artefacts on disk. Generation metadata saves alongside each video metadata, and the file system is "arguably the most foundational harness primitive" for persistence across runs files. A Kaggle session is ephemeral; your prompt/seed/clip ledger should not be.

Gaps — what this corpus cannot tell you

These are the questions you asked, or will ask next, that twenty talks do not answer. Do not let anyone — including a model — fill them in for you.

  • Google Colab specifically. The only Colab mention is that the creator's repo holds "notebooks for Collab and Kaggle" mention. Nothing in the corpus states Colab's free GPU allocation, runtime or disk limits, whether a free Colab session offers two GPUs, or whether the H3 notebook has ever been run there. Your single most load-bearing question is the one the corpus answers least.
  • Single-GPU MiniMax H3. Every figure assumes the transformer sharded across two T4s at 9.3 GB each. No video shows a single-card configuration, a CPU-offload path, or a quantised H3 variant.
  • StoryMem's speed and runtime. The corpus gives parameter count, training-clip count and VRAM footprint, but no generation latency, no per-shot render time, and no end-to-end wall-clock for a one-minute story. You cannot budget a production schedule from it.
  • MiniMax H3 + StoryMem together. StoryMem is documented as a LoRA for Wan 2.2-based models backbone. No video states whether it composes with MiniMax H3, or with H3's four-step distillation LoRAs. Treat "H3 plus StoryMem" as an untested hypothesis.
  • Independent benchmark validation. PDMD's leaderboard position, DMAD's 79% win rate and StoryMem's 28.7%/9.4% consistency gains are all vendor-published. The corpus contains no third-party replication of any of them.
  • Dollar costs for GPU rental. No video prices a cloud GPU hour, an RTX 5090, or the per-clip cost of rendering at scale. The nearest cost signals in the corpus are for text models and agentic coding, not video.
  • Training your own video LoRA. All LoRA hyperparameters, timings and dataset-size figures in this corpus come from text-model fine-tunes. No video shows building a video-model style LoRA, nor the data engineering that turns raw clips into training pairs.
  • What to do when an unattended render dies. No video covers recovery from a mid-render session crash, a timed-out tunnel, or a notebook killed by the free tier's limits — beyond noting that Gradio links crash and that scheduled jobs need the host to stay on.
  • Automated video evaluation. The eval material in this corpus is all text-output grading. Nothing covers an automated judge for video — no metric for character consistency, cut obedience, or face integrity that you could run instead of watching clips yourself.
  • Rights, licensing and platform policy. No video addresses the licensing terms of these open weights, the rules for monetising AI-generated video on YouTube, or disclosure requirements for synthetic voices and faces.
