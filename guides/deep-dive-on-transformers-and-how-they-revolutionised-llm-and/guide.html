The sequence bottleneck, and the architecture that broke it

What the transformer actually changed about neural network training, how it differs from RNNs and LSTMs mechanism by mechanism, and what that change costs you downstream — distilled from Stanford's CME295 lectures (Afshine and Shervine Amidi), Maxime Labonne, Sebastian Raschka, Cormac Brick, Sunny Savita, Dave Ebbelaar, KodeKloud and the Databricks/MosaicML keynote.

Thesis: attention traded memory for wiring

The short answer to the question. An RNN carries the meaning of a sentence forward in a single hidden vector, one token at a time; the transformer deletes that vector and gives every token a direct link to every other token at once. The 2017 paper's whole bet was to move away from sequential processing and instead let the model have direct connections with all parts of the text at once. Three consequences fall out of that one change, and they are the reason the architecture took over: the gradient no longer has to survive a long multiplicative chain through time, the representation of a word becomes context-specific, and the computation becomes a pile of matrix multiplications instead of a loop — GPUs love matrices.

That is the mechanism. The revolution in training is a second-order effect: once the forward pass is parallel matrix math, you can spend compute instead of wall-clock. An RNN had to compute all the hidden states before predicting a word, so long sequences meant long training; the transformer runs the whole sequence in one pass and runs its attention heads with all of that done in parallel. Scale became a budget line rather than a schedule: a modern pre-training run lands around 1025 FLOPs and tens of millions of dollars. The same bet that bought parallelism is also what bills you: self-attention is n² in sequence length, and most of the serving stack you will ever touch exists to pay that bill.

The corpus is blunt about the one thing beginners over-read: the architecture is not where the intelligence lives. The transformer is only a page of code, and GPT, Claude and Llama all run essentially that same formula — what differs is only the learned weight values, which are numbers learned from examples. Read this guide as architecture first, then as the training and serving consequences that architecture forces on you.

“Even from the title, you can see that the authors wanted to just rely on that part.” — on the paper Attention is All You Need, 2017.

Delete the hidden state

In an RNN the sentence's meaning is solely encapsulated into this hidden state. Attention replaces that single channel with a direct link per token pair, so nothing has to be remembered through a bottleneck.

Make it a matrix

Expressing the computation as query/key/value matrices lets the whole sequence be attended in one matrix multiply — made for the hardware we have, which does about 100× more math per second than a CPU.

Pay the n² tax on purpose

Activation memory now scales with how large your context length is. Flash attention, KV caching and prefill/decode splitting are all downstream of that single trade.

Lineage: forty years to get to one page of code

The corpus dates the field back well before ChatGPT and is precise about why the early ideas stalled: the models existed, the inputs did not. In the 1980s and 1990s, there was not a lot of compute and no internet-scale corpus. Read the timeline as a sequence of patches, each fixing the previous one's failure, until 2017 removed the thing being patched.

1

1980s — recurrence introduces word order

The first real answer to "a bag of embeddings loses the order of the words."

  • An RNN processes tokens one at a time and keeps a hidden representation of the sentence so far, variously called the hidden state, activation or context vector.
  • The win was real: with RNNs the word order matters, and the same machinery served classification (project the last hidden state), tagging, and generation.
  • Blocker of the era: no internet and no compute, which is explicitly called out as one of the limiting factors that kept today's models untrainable then.
2

1990s — the LSTM bolts on a second memory

Patch one: if one hidden state forgets, add a channel whose job is not forgetting.

  • Long Short-Term Memory adds another tracked quantity called the cell state on top of the hidden state.
  • The lecture's verdict on the patch is flat: it aims at improving that piece, but it was not perfect either — the forgetting problem stayed the main issue of RNN-based methods.
3

2013 — Word2vec proves representations can be learned

Before you can attend over tokens you need vectors worth attending over.

  • Word2vec learned embeddings from a proxy task and produced interpretable analogies — king is to queen what this is to that.
  • Dimensions sit in the hundreds, far below vocabulary size: 768 is one commonly cited example, against a vocabulary of tens of thousands of tokens for one language.
  • Its fatal limit, and the exact gap attention closes: Word2vec and FastText produce static embeddings with no awareness of context.
4

2014 — attention arrives as an RNN accessory

Patch two: stop relying on memory, add a shortcut to the past.

  • Attention gives a direct link between what you are trying to predict and something earlier in the text; it was introduced in 2014, three years before the transformer.
  • It was invented specifically to solve for these long-range dependency issues — the RNN's defect, not a new capability.
5

2017 — the paper removes the thing being patched

If the shortcut is what works, keep the shortcut and throw away the loop.

  • The transformer, introduced in a paper named Attention is All You Need, is at the foundation of all of the models you see today.
  • To build the representation of a token the model will look at all the other tokens in the sequence at once — this is self-attention.
  • The attention mechanism itself is not exotic: it is a weight-oriented mechanism, deciding which word matters to which other word.
6

2020s — the same block, scaled until it became an LLM

No architecture change was needed to get from translation to ChatGPT; only data and compute.

  • The models were scaled up both by compute and in terms of the data — and that is how LLMs were dubbed.
  • The scale delta is visible in two numbers: GPT-3 trained on 300 billion tokens, Llama 3 on 15 trillion.
  • Over 90% of modern LLMs built this way are decoder-only models — the 2017 encoder-decoder got cut in half along the way.
  • Adoption numbers from the same era: in one 30-day window, more than 1,500 customers used a Transformer model on Databricks, with GPU usage growing 25% month over month.

The recurrence tax: four bills an RNN hands you

If you only remember one framing for the RNN-versus-transformer question, make it this: recurrence is not wrong, it is expensive in four specific ways, and attention pays off all four at once. Each card below is the failure as the corpus states it, not a paraphrase of textbook lore.

1. The gradient dies on the way back

Predicting a late word depends on every hidden state before it, so updating weights means back-propagating through time. Written out, that is a product of many quantities: if it's less than 1, it's vanishing — multiply enough of those and the update goes to zero. The literature name for it is the vanishing gradient.

2. One vector has to hold the whole sentence

Because the meaning is solely encapsulated into this hidden state, long inputs degrade the model's ability to "remember" what it saw. This is what people mean by long-range dependencies; the LSTM's cell state was an attempt, not a fix.

3. Training is serial, so GPUs idle

Stated plainly in the lecture: the computations are very slow, because at training time you need to compute all these hidden states before the token you want. Longer sequence, linearly longer wall-clock, with no way to spend parallel hardware on it.

4. Word representations are position-blind

The pre-attention era's representations were token-specific regardless of where they're at. Self-attention fixes exactly this: in "river bank" versus "robbing a bank", the bank would have different representations — and the same contrast is why sentence-transformer embeddings beat Word2vec, since bank will be represented according to the context.

The swap, mechanism by mechanism

Use this table when someone asks for the difference in an interview or a design review. Left column is the recurrent mechanism; right column is what the transformer put in its place, and what that move bought or cost.

Recurrent mechanismWhat the transformer put there instead
Hidden state passed forward token by token All-to-all self-attention: look at all the other tokens at once, with no memory bottleneck to survive.
Order comes free from the loop Order must be injected. Because the links are direct, unlike the RNN you lose the sense of order, so you add position encodings to inform on the position.
Sequential forward and backward passes One matrix-multiply pass over the sequence; heads run with all of that done in parallel, then concatenated and projected back.
Memory cost roughly linear in sequence length Memory cost quadratic: n² complexity because of this self attention operation. You traded time for space, deliberately.
Static, context-free token embeddings feeding the loop Context-specific representations computed from the whole sequence, so each token's representation is unique to its context.
Hand-built task architectures per NLP task One stack, pre-trained once then adapted: a pre-training stage followed by cheap tuning, instead of training from scratch per task.

Read the block in one pass

Six steps, in the order the data moves. This is the original encoder-decoder shape — an encoder on the left side and a decoder — built around translation. Decoder-only LLMs keep steps 1–3, 5 and 6, and keep only the masked flavour of step 4.

1

Tokenize

Models understand numbers, not text, so cut the text first.

  • Tokenization is cutting the text with respect to some arbitrary unit of text; that unit is the token.
  • Subword is the default middle ground because it works by leveraging roots of words, so related words share structure — at the cost of longer sequences.
  • Word-level carries the worst out-of-vocabulary risk — this thing is called OOV — while character-level eliminates it and inflates sequence length.
  • Rough serving conversion to keep in your head: a token is roughly 3/4 of a word.
2

Embed

Turn each token id into a dense vector the network can move around.

  • An embedding is a dense high-dimensional numerical vector representing semantic meaning; common sizes run 384, 768, 1024, 1536, 2048.
  • Target vocabulary size is tens of thousands for one language, hundreds of thousands for multilingual or code-covering models.
3

Add position encodings

The step that exists only because recurrence was removed.

  • Direct token-to-token links destroy the implicit ordering a loop gave you, so you add position encodings to inform on the position of the word in the sequence.
  • The lecture flags it and skips the formula — we're not going to dig into that today — so treat the mechanism as understood and the math as a gap (see Gaps).
4

Multi-head attention (three flavours)

The same layer does three different jobs depending on where the queries and keys come from.

  • Encoder self-attention computes embeddings from the input sentence as a function of themselves.
  • Decoder self-attention is masked — it only looks at the tokens already decoded, never to the right of the one being predicted.
  • Cross-attention expresses the output as a function of what has been seen in the input.
5

Feed-forward expansion

Where Word2vec compressed, the transformer expands — on purpose.

  • Unlike Word2vec's bottleneck, here the hidden layer is of a bigger dimension than input and output, to buy degrees of freedom for richer features.
  • This block matters more than its reputation suggests for adaptation: analysis after the LoRA paper found that the bulk of the performance improvements is actually contained in the feed-forward block, not the attention matrices the paper originally targeted.
6

Stack N, then softmax over the vocabulary

Depth is repetition, not new machinery — which is also why parameter counts explode.

  • You get a stacked succession of N encoders and N decoders; the final encoder representation is what is fed to the N decoders' cross-attention.
  • At the end you project to a vector of vocabulary size and softmax it to guess what the next word is.
  • Stacking is why weight counts balloon: with blocks repeated, attention plus feed-forward weights land in the billions — it's going to be in billions, with individual matrices at 512×512 and bigger.
  • One training trick from the original paper is worth copying: instead of a hard one-hot target, label smoothing says predict this word, but there's a chance it's not this word, because there is rarely only one valid continuation.

The attention computation, line by line

Four operations. If you can write these from memory you can read any transformer diagram. Nothing here is more than a linear projection, a dot product, a softmax and a weighted sum — it is a matrix game end to end.

1

Project each token three ways

Query, key and value are not three kinds of data — they are three learned views of the same vector.

  • Inside multi-head attention the input is multiplied with the three main matrices, Q, K and V, as a linear transformation.
  • The projection matrices are what the word "head" names: the word head is given to the projection matrices used to obtain query, key and value.
2

Score query against every key, then scale

This is the "direct link" from the thesis, expressed as a dot product.

  • You compare the query to the key to quantify how similar they are, for every pair in the sequence at once.
  • Dot products grow with dimension, so you normalize them — which is why you divide by the square root of the dimension of keys.
  • A worked intuition from the LoRA walkthrough: in "I love machine learning" the attention weight ties learning to machine — it belongs to the machine only, not love.
3

Softmax, then take a weighted sum of values

The output of attention is always a convex mixture of other tokens' value vectors.

  • Softmax over the scaled scores gives a probability distribution of the projection of the query over keys, one row per query.
  • Multiply by V and you end up with a weighted sum of values for each query: weight the most similar keys and take the corresponding value.
  • Self-attention's job, in one sentence from the practitioner side: it is trying to check the relation between every pair of words in the same sentence.
4

Run h heads in parallel, concatenate, project back

The cheapest capacity increase in the architecture, and the one that makes it GPU-shaped.

  • Multiple heads mean multiple learned projections, allowing your model to learn different projections and therefore different token-to-token associations. If you have a vision background, it is like having multiple filters in a convolution.
  • The h head outputs are computed with all of that done in parallel, concatenated columnwise, then pushed through an output projection Wo back to the embedding dimension.
  • Nothing forces the heads to differ; they differ because of the magic of gradient descent — the network has no incentive to copy itself.
  • Why this shape and not another: matrix form means the whole-sequence computation is matrix multiplication, and GPUs love matrices — a GPU's thousands of small cores do about 100 times more math every second than a CPU's few big ones.

The n² bill, and the six things built to pay it

Everything in this section is a consequence of deleting the hidden state. An RNN's memory cost grew with depth in time; a transformer's grows with the square of context. Training-side mitigations and serving-side mitigations are different engineering problems with the same root cause, so learn both.

Where training memory actually goes

A training step must hold activations, gradients and optimizer moments at once, and activation memory depends on model size, batch size and context length — because it's dependent on how large your context length is, at n² complexity for the self attention operation. The transformer-era default optimizer is AdamW, which combines the weight decay concept with a warm-up schedule.

Flash attention: tile it into fast memory

Flash attention, developed at Stanford in 2022, exploits the gap between large-slow HBM and small-fast SRAM. The method is called tiling: compute softmax exactly on small blocks end to end in SRAM instead of writing giant intermediates back to HBM. Reported effect is almost a 10× reduction in HBM reads and writes, and it saves memory and runtime at the same time.

Spread it: data, model, ZeRO

Data parallelism distributes data across GPUs with a full model copy each, paying gradient-averaging traffic. Zero redundancy optimization shards optimizer states, gradients and parameters instead of duplicating them. Model parallelism adds expert parallelism, tensor parallelism and pipeline parallelism.

Lower the precision

Quantization halves footprint and roughly doubles throughput: dropping FP32 to FP16 means it takes basically half the memory. The byte arithmetic is worth memorising — FP32 is around 4 bytes per weight, FP16 about 2, and 4-bit around 0.5 bytes. Mixed-precision training keeps master weights in FP32 while running the passes in FP16.

At inference: cache the prefill

Generation is one trick repeated — it predicts the next token, appends it and goes again — so a 100-token answer is 100 passes. The prompt is read in one compute-heavy burst whose latency is TTFT, time to first token; that work is saved so each later token reaches into the cache and reuses everything it already worked out. Keep those blocks past the end of a request and you get prefix caching, where a cached input token costs about a tenth of a fresh one.

Decode is bandwidth-bound, so batch and shard

Each decode step rereads the whole model from VRAM, so a 3 TB/s GPU on a 16 GB model tops out: 200 tokens a second is the maximum limit. The fix is batching, since one read gives one token for one user or 50 tokens for 50 users. Concurrency then caps on memory, not math — it's the memory that decides how many people one GPU can serve. When weights exceed one card (a 70B model is 140 GB against an 80 GB H100), you resort to splitting a model across GPUs and keep the chatty talk inside a box.

Two routing pitfalls follow directly from the KV cache being physical state on a specific machine. A plain round-robin balancer throws away saved work that was perfectly good; cache-aware routing instead delivers around three times the throughput on identical hardware, and splitting compute-heavy prefill from bandwidth-heavy decode pools yields up to 70% more tokens per second on the same hardware. If you are self-hosting, note that vLLM speaks the exact same format the OpenAI API uses, so the swap is a base-URL change.

How the architecture rewrote the training pipeline

This is the part of "revolutionised neural network training" that is not about attention math. Because one expensive pre-training run produces a general model, training became a staged transfer-learning pipeline rather than a per-task project — a pre-training stage followed by cheaper tuning. In the corpus's terminology, fine-tuning plus preference tuning is what we call alignment, with a newer mid-training stage emerging between them.

1

Pre-train: next-token prediction at internet scale

One objective, one corpus, one enormous bill — and a base model that cannot hold a conversation.

  • The first stage is pre-training, where the model learns to do next-token prediction.
  • Chinchilla's compute-optimal rule: use a training set about 20 times the model size in tokens — by which GPT-3 (175B parameters, 300B tokens) was undertrained.
  • Cost floor is millions and it can even cost tens of millions of dollars, plus a tracked ecological cost.
  • The output is bounded in time: a base model's knowledge stops at what is called the knowledge cutoff date, and injecting new facts afterward without damaging other capabilities is unsolved.
  • The resulting model is not an assistant: it has been trained on next token prediction, not being an assistant, so it will autocomplete your question instead of answering it.
  • A counterweight on cost from the enterprise side: MosaicML's MPT-7B was trained from scratch where it only costs 250,000 to build it, versus the 10 to 15 million dollars cited for GPT-3-scale, and MPT-30B at seven or eight hundred thousand dollars.
2

Supervised fine-tune: same loss, different text

The most misunderstood stage. SFT does not change the objective; it changes what you compute the loss over.

  • SFT is short for supervised fine-tuning: keep training the pre-trained model on input/output pairs so it predicts the right tokens for a given input.
  • It reuses the pre-training objective exactly — instruction fine-tuning uses exactly the same loss that we used during pre-training.
  • The loss is masked to the response: we don't put a loss over the input, and in practice you train on the outputs only.
  • Mechanically, replace target padding with -100 — replacing all the target padding tokens by the minus 100, PyTorch's ignore index — and pad only to the longest element in each batch, not the dataset.
  • Dataset scale is orders of magnitude below pre-training: about 13k for GPT-3 and 10 million for Llama 3; a practical floor for real instruction tuning is at least 100,000 training examples.
  • Template length is a real cost line, not a style choice: shorter prompt templates mean fewer tokens per example, and shorter is cheaper.
  • Hardware sets the iteration loop: the same 355M-parameter 2-epoch run that takes 15–29 minutes on a laptop finishes in about a minute on an A100 GPU.
3

Preference-tune: teach tone, not facts

The stage that made transformers usable as products, and the one with the worst cost/complexity curve.

  • The unit of data is a rejected output paired with a preferred rewrite for the same prompt — a preference pair — collected pairwise because pointwise scoring is hard to calibrate.
  • RLHF is two stages: fit a reward model using the Bradley-Terry formulation, then optimise the policy with RL.
  • The RL objective must optimise for higher rewards without going too far from the SFT reference, via a KL penalty — otherwise you get reward hacking against an imperfect proxy.
  • PPO keeps updates close to the previous policy with a clipped objective, but you need a lot of models resident: policy, reference, reward model, value function.
  • DPO collapses that into one loss on preference pairs, so instead of four models you just need two — it is simpler than RLHF because it does not use a separate reward model.
  • The honest trade: overall PPO performs better than DPO, but the RL bar is very hard to tune right. Pick by whether you have RL expertise to spend.
  • If you want the reward signal without the training loop, best-of-N scores N samples from a frozen model — but you still wait more than a single pass, so it trades cost for latency rather than removing either.
4

Post-2024: RL on verifiable rewards, bolted onto the same block

Reasoning models are not a new architecture. They are the same transformer with a different reward and more decode tokens.

  • Vanilla next-token models have quote-unquote limited reasoning and are all talk, no action — they can only emit text.
  • Chain-of-thought works because a hard problem is unlikely to appear verbatim in training data, so you make the model decompose the problem into tractable ones; letting it emit more tokens first means you just give it more compute.
  • The reward needs no reward model at all for math and code: combine a format check — checking whether the think tokens are here — with a correctness check against tests or ground truth.
  • GRPO drops PPO's value function entirely, computing advantage as reward minus the average over the standard deviation across a sampled group of completions.
  • Known bug worth knowing: GRPO's per-token length normalisation pushes outputs longer and longer; Dr. GRPO removes the factor altogether.
  • Pure RL on a base model works but degrades the chain: R1-Zero would mix languages and had syntax issues, which is why R1 added cold-start SFT data and a language-consistency reward.
  • For small models, distilling the knowledge is more efficient than running RL on them from scratch.

One structural warning that applies to every number in this section. Benchmark comparisons across models are only meaningful if both models had comparable exposure to the benchmark's domain — the documented phenomenon is training on the test task, not the test set, the test task. Crowd leaderboards are not a refuge either: it's easily possible to rig such a leaderboard, and when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure. MMLU in particular mostly measures how well pre-training was done, not reasoning.

Adapt, do not retrain: the architecture's other gift

LoRA is usually taught as a memory trick. It is better understood as a direct exploitation of the transformer's shape: the model is a stack of large weight matrices, and the update to one of those matrices turns out to be low-rank. Full fine-tuning updates everything — a 13 billion model means 13 billion weights — so we cannot perform full-parameter fine-tuning on a single GPU.

The low-rank bet

LoRA's assumption, stated plainly: it is possible that the weight update matrix is not full rank. A rank-2 4×5 matrix (20 numbers) factors into 4×2 and 2×5 (18 numbers); at scale, billions of parameters become millions. The rank r is typically taken very small against W0's hundreds or thousands of dimensions.

Where the adapters attach

You freeze all the pre-trained weights and add adapters, then append the adapter onto the multi-head attention matrices and the feed-forward layer of each block. Target choice is itself a hyperparameter: Q alone is average, Q plus V is better, and the feed-forward block is where the bulk of the performance improvements is actually contained.

The numbers that justify it

On GPT-3, LoRA trains only 4.7 million parameters against 175 billion, and the cited comparison has full fine-tuning at 89.5% accuracy with LoRA matching it. In the worked walkthrough, rank 16 and alpha 32 leave 0.277% of parameters trainable.

QLoRA: quantize the frozen half

QLoRA quantizes the pre-trained model in 4-bit precision — NF4 means Normal Float 4 — while the adapters train at higher precision, bringing a task down to 8 to 16 GB of VRAM. It is a real trade, not a free lunch: lower VRAM, degraded performance. Stack-wise you add bitsandbytes for the quantization on top of peft, which implements the LoRA.

Hyperparameters that behave differently

LoRA is not full fine-tuning with fewer weights; its dynamics differ. Empirically you want higher learning rates — 10 times more is the guidance — and it does not perform as well with larger batch size. For full fine-tuning the search procedure is blunt: go as high as you can until your loss explodes, then back off.

Merging: capability without gradients

Because every fine-tune of a given base shares a weight geometry, you can average them. Merging does not require any GPU, and on the Open LLM Leaderboard's 7B tier the entire top eight or ten was merge models. SLERP — spherical linear interpolation — merges two; DARE prunes and rescales and works really well with seven or eight models; or extract feed-forward layers from several fine-tunes and add a router for a FrankenMoE.

At the small end, the same architecture runs entirely on a phone. Models smaller than a billion parameters hit almost 2,000 tokens per second prefill and 140 decode on an old Pixel 7, and fine-tuning moved one 270M function-calling model from 46% to over 90% app-intent accuracy. The constraint is narrowness: at that size it needs to have a very narrow and focused task.

Gaps: what these thirteen talks do not answer

Read this list before you treat the guide as complete. Each item is something a reader of this topic would reasonably ask and the corpus does not support — stated here rather than filled in from memory.

  • No position-encoding math. Lecture 1 names position encodings and explicitly declines the formula — so you get why they exist, not how sinusoidal (or any other) encoding is computed.
  • No layer norm, no residual connections. Two load-bearing components of every real transformer block are absent from the whole corpus. The LoRA walkthrough stays at the Q/K/V and feed-forward level by its own admission.
  • No head-splitting math. "h heads with different projection matrices, concatenated and projected back" is as deep as it goes; how d_model divides across heads is never shown.
  • No measured transformer-vs-LSTM head-to-head. The displacement is argued from mechanism — vanishing gradients, slow sequential compute, context-blind representations — and from the 2017 paper's translation results. No corpus talk reports a score delta on the same task.
  • No modern architectural variants. RoPE, grouped-query attention, sliding-window attention and MoE routing internals are never covered. Multi-head latent attention and MoE are named once in passing in the DeepSeek-V3 context and not explained.
  • No counter-argument to attention. Nothing in the corpus asks whether recurrence is returning — linear attention and state-space models (Mamba and relatives) do not appear at all.
  • No link from architecture choices to KV-cache sizing. Context length, head count and attention variant drive KV-cache memory directly, but the serving talk treats the cache purely as an ops concern and never gives a sizing formula.
  • No cost figures for the alignment stages. Pre-training is quantified in dollars and FLOPs; RLHF/PPO and GRPO runs are never given wall-clock or dollar numbers, and the DeepSeek R1 cold-start dataset size is explicitly unknown.
  • No procedure for choosing LoRA rank or alpha. Rank 16 and alpha 32 appear as one demo's values. Nothing in the corpus tells you how to pick them for a new task, and QLoRA gets no perplexity or accuracy numbers of its own.
