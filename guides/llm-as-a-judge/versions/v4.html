The thesis: narrow it, calibrate it, characterise its error

Everyone reaches for LLM-as-a-judge for the same reason. Deterministic code evals work only when the success criteria are clear-cut and the data is well structured code evals, and human annotation — hands-down the best signal you can collect — is either sparse or slow and expensive human evals. The judge is the middle ground: the thing you use when a task is too ambiguous for a regex and too costly for a person the gap. And it does buy real speed — with prompt tuning and the right model you get to roughly 90% of what you need very fast fast start.

The failure is always the same too. The dev loop is bottlenecked on evaluation, so you swap the human for a judge and the loop gets fast — but if the judge doesn't correlate with human annotation you end up with useless signal and the loop "won't go anywhere" useless signal. A first-pass judge rates every sampled output "good" everything good; a naive seed judge scores 61% and calls the trajectory compliant 98% of the time, which is just the majority class with extra steps majority collapse. The most common mistake in the field is assuming the judge works out of the box assume nothing.

Before you create a judge, you have to be the judge. Evidently AI

The corpus converges on one reframing that makes everything else follow. An evaluator is a measurement instrument with a failure profile, not simply a scoring function instrument. Instruments get narrowed so they can be calibrated, calibrated against a reference you trust, and characterised — sensitivity, specificity, bias, repeatability — before anyone reads a number off them. Three moves, in that order.

1 · Narrow it

You do not need an overall judge for your product; you can create multiple small judges where each evaluates one specific thing many small judges. Netflix builds a prompt per criterion because one overloaded prompt degrades quality per-criteria judges. Combining concerns in one judge makes a failure impossible to diagnose one decision.

2 · Calibrate it

Compare judge labels to human labels on a golden set and compute exact-match accuracy — human alignment — then read the disagreements and fix the prompt human alignment. It is a five-step loop: predictions, critiques, human labels, comparison, iterate the loop.

3 · Characterise it

Report TP/FP/TN/FN — true and false positives and negatives, defined below — with sensitivity, specificity and balanced accuracy instead of one headline number confusion matrix, test the four known biases bias card, and re-run — one successful trace does not establish a reliable workflow repeatability.

The prize for doing this is compounding, not linear. Getting through four eval iterations in a month instead of two produces an exponentially better product iteration velocity. The scale is real: Arize runs over 100 million evals a month, the average customer runs about 12 eval jobs, and the top teams run more than 3,800 distinct evaluators eval scale, with Duolingo running roughly 20 evals per trace 20 per trace.

The four cells, in plain terms

TP, FP, TN and FN are the four cells of a confusion matrix: your human labels crosstabulated against the judge's calls, one cell per combination. Name the positive class before you compute anything — the class you pick decides how every prediction and every error is interpreted name the positive class. Take the worked example's positive class — "the human label says this response contains an unsupported claim", so a positive prediction means the judge flagged it:

  • TP — true positive. The label says unsupported and the judge flags the response. A catch true positive.
  • FP — false positive. The response is supported and the judge flags it anyway. A false alarm; it costs human review capacity and can suppress a useful answer false positive.
  • TN — true negative. The response is supported and the judge passes it. Correctly left alone true negative.
  • FN — false negative. The response contains an unsupported claim and the judge passes it false negative. This is the dangerous cell: the ungrounded statement reaches the user, exactly where the evaluator was supposed to be the safety signal the cost of a miss.

The rates are just ratios of those cells. Sensitivity (TPR) = TP / (TP + FN) is the proportion of genuinely unsupported responses the judge flags sensitivity. Specificity (TNR) = TN / (TN + FP) is the proportion of supported responses it leaves unflagged specificity. Balanced accuracy is the mean of the two, giving equal weight to the positive and negative classes balanced accuracy. That is why the headline number is not enough: raw agreement can be high when unsupported claims are rare even while TPR is poor why not accuracy. Stage 07 puts numbers in the cells — of 200 held-out responses, TP 34, FN 6, FP 16, TN 144, which is where TPR 0.85, TNR 0.90 and balanced accuracy 0.875 come from the four counts.

The build: seven stages from raw traces to a release gate

Every talk in the corpus that actually shipped a judge walks some version of this path. Skipping stages 1–3 is why judges fail; the teams who rushed metric design and annotation all report those as the hardest part in hindsight data is hardest.

01

Look at your data before you define anything

You cannot write criteria for failure modes you have not seen. This stage is deliberately informal.

  • Observe real inputs and outputs and note failure modes — no formal annotation yet. "This is more of a vibe check." vibe check
  • But a vibe check alone is not enough; it doesn't tell you the whole story and you need rigorous methods and metrics on top of it not sufficient.
  • Sample real traffic — especially downvoted or rejected responses — rather than synthetic data or public benchmarks real traffic. Public benchmarks decay because they leak into training data leakage.
  • Topic-cluster your logged queries before you decide what to evaluate. One client found around 80% of their questions fell into a narrow subset of topics 80/20.
  • This never stops, and it is not beneath you: a principal engineer at Zapier spends three hours per day looking at traces and data three hours a day, and manual inspection has the highest value-to-prestige ratio of any eval activity value to prestige.
  • Read transcripts, not just scores — that is how you catch a support agent telling a user their 45-minute-old order had passed a 60-minute cancellation window arithmetic failure.
02

Derive the metric from a failure, not from a template

Generic criteria produce generic results. The metric is the product decision; everything downstream is plumbing.

  • Start with a failure, not with a favour evaluator — a general-purpose judge will happily report that your assistant improved while it is quietly rewarding length start from failure.
  • Metrics must be specific, answerable and actionable — finer-grained beats general, a human must be able to label it from the context, and a low score must point at what to fix three tests.
  • Kill the catch-all. One metric called "quality" gives the judge nothing to focus on and gives you no direction when it drops no catch-all. A single omnibus score cannot show whether retrieval failed, extraction over-generated, or the evaluator just prefers verbosity omnibus.
  • Kill the sprawl too. Ten overlapping metrics — fluency, style, tone, politeness — waste tokens and cause cognitive overload, and LLMs are already fluent out of the box metric sprawl.
  • Derive criteria from the business use case, ideally with subject-matter experts, rather than reaching for "hallucination" from the use case. Error analysis on real support transcripts surfaced four distinct error types — policy adherence, response style, information delivery, tool-call correctness — and each wants its own judge four error types.
  • Define the positive class explicitly. A claim is not positive merely because it is plausible; and decide your unit of analysis — claim-level and response-level are different measurements positive class.
  • Write the spec to a machine-readable file and have a repository test load it, confirm the positive class is declared, and reject an empty evidence contract tested spec.
  • For a new RAG app, four or five metrics are the right starting set: response relevance, response completeness, context relevance, hallucination/groundedness, conversation satisfaction RAG starter set.
03

Be the judge yourself — build the golden set

Annotating does double duty: it proves your guidelines are unambiguous, and it gives you the reference the judge is measured against.

  • Both purposes matter. If you cannot unambiguously label your own data on your own criteria, the LLM will struggle too; and the labels are what let you quantify how well the judge performs two jobs.
  • Use binary or very few classes. A 1–10 scale is hard to apply consistently; binary labels make annotation far easier binary labels. Netflix names the switch from a 1–4 Likert scale to pass/fail as a key change that lifted inter-annotator agreement Likert to binary.
  • Record why, not just the label. Without the annotator's reasoning, a prompt-optimisation algorithm has no way to recover the underlying rule reasons required.
  • Run two graders against the same rubric. Frequent disagreement means the rubric is ambiguous, not that the graders are bad rubric diagnostic.
  • Treat calibration as iterative: annotate, measure disagreement, refine written guidelines with concrete examples, repeat. Netflix collected roughly 1,000 synopsis evaluations this way calibration rounds, landing a 600-item golden set sampled uniformly across content categories and genres for diversity rather than raw count diversity over size.
  • Sizing, honestly: around 10 examples is enough to decide whether to invest further start at 10, roughly 100+ before production depending on your regulatory bar 100 for production, and 50–100 samples is a good starting test set 50–100. Do not let sizing stop you: "five is better than nothing" start tiny.
  • Balance the classes so you can see both error directions — the code-review study used 27 bad and 23 good examples balanced set.
  • Deliberately include hard cases — typos, long threads, conflicting fields, negation, near-duplicates, correct-but-less-verbose answers — because random sampling alone can produce an easy test hard cases.
  • Split the labels before you touch the judge prompt. One worked allocation: 108 for judge design, 36 for development checks, 36 sealed for the final test frozen split.
04

Write the judge prompt as guidelines for a human

Keep it minimal, keep it one decision, and make the output machine-checkable at the API level rather than by pleading in prose.

  • The standard anatomy: role, task description, evaluation criteria, a scoring rubric per criterion, optional few-shot rubric examples, the candidate output, explicit instructions, and a structured JSON output spec prompt anatomy.
  • Scope it to one decision and explicitly exclude helpfulness or writing quality if that is not what you are measuring one decision, and use explicit labels like supported / unsupported / not_verifiable instead of vague scores explicit labels.
  • Ask only what a model can actually assess. "Is this answer medically correct?" is out of scope; "is this answer based on the context?" is in scope scope limits.
  • Cap the judge's context at 4,000 tokens or less — even million-token models struggle to reason over the full length, so trim rather than dumping your knowledge base in 4K cap. The rule comes from a benchmark where reasoning performance dropped steeply after about 4,000 tokens regardless of advertised window the study.
  • Bound the evidence. Give the judge the task instruction, the retrieved records and the target output in delimited fields — never hidden application metadata or the variant's name evidence contract.
  • Demand reasoning before the score. Rationale-first mirrors chain-of-thought and empirically improves the judgment rationale first; requiring a rationale makes judges better at the whole task, not just more explainable accuracy gain. Adding the single line "Always explain your reasoning" to an already-detailed prompt moved accuracy from 95% to 98% one extra line.
  • Write general criteria, not your examples. Pasting labelled examples in makes the judge score those correctly and fail to generalise no memorisation. In the GEPA study, a seed prompt that did not contain the agent's policy text beat one that did, because pasting it trapped the optimiser in a local minimum policy trap.
  • Tell it not to suggest improvements. Left unsaid, judges default to giving improvement feedback instead of a clean label, which breaks parsing no advice.
  • Force structure at the API, not in prose. Prompting alone does not guarantee a parseable response — use constrained/guided decoding and structured output guided decoding, and stop spending tokens telling the judge to "respond in exactly one word" skip format prose.
  • Reject bad output loudly. A parser that rejects unknown labels, missing keys or malformed evidence arrays must trigger retry or review — never a silent pass strict parsing. Without an explicit structure constraint, a judge LLM can return a response of any shape any shape.
  • A little domain knowledge in the prompt goes a long way domain knowledge, and punish the things you don't want: the UPSC grading prompt explicitly instructs the judge not to reward verbosity or keyword stuffing no padding.
05

Choose the judge model — and don't let it grade itself

Judge quality is prompt and model together. The corpus disagrees on tier; it does not disagree on family.

  • Never use the same model for the application and its judge. Intuitively it is "letting a student grade their own essay" student grading themselves; formally it is self-enhancement bias, where a model prefers responses generated by itself self-enhancement, and the remedy is a different model for generation and judging different family.
  • Model tier is not free. The same winning prompt on GPT-3.5 Turbo instead of GPT-4 mini collapsed to 48% recall and 72% accuracy tier collapse, and in the GEPA study small or older models as judge or refiner were "a complete failure" on complex-policy work small models fail.
  • Where the corpus splits: use the most powerful model you can afford most powerful versus don't use an expensive frontier model for routine evaluation because the cost adds up cheap judge. The workable synthesis from the GEPA run: a bigger model for the refinement/reflection step, a smaller cheaper model for the judge itself, because the judge runs at online volume big refiner, small judge.
  • Don't start with reasoning models. Their extra reasoning overhead is often overkill for eval when the metrics are well defined skip reasoning models. Bigger and proprietary models do tend to align better with humans alignment scaling.
  • Consider the cheaper tier entirely. Encoder-only (BERT-style) evaluators run about 10× cheaper and one to two orders of magnitude faster than judge calls encoder-only, and a trained classifier sits between rules and a judge — people skip it because of a lack of labelled data, not because it doesn't work classifiers.
  • Run the judge cold. Low temperature (roughly 0.0–0.2) keeps scores reproducible across repeated runs low temperature, and pin candidate models at 0.0 so the same question returns the same answer deterministic candidates.
  • Speed is a selection criterion too: with the same winning prompt, Claude performed nearly as well as GPT-4 mini as a judge but OpenAI ran noticeably faster latency differs.
06

Meta-evaluate: measure the judge against your labels

This is the step that converts a plausible prompt into a trusted instrument. It is also the step teams skip.

  • The simplest version: exact-match accuracy of judge labels against human labels, then read the judge's explanations on the disagreements and roll the fix into a new evaluator version meta-eval.
  • Track agreement as an explicit metric. A first-pass judge hit only 70% agreement with human labels on 10 examples 70% first pass.
  • Prefer classification metrics over a single correlation number: accuracy, precision and recall are intuitive and let you weight the error direction that matters classification metrics.
  • Watch for a judge that is precise but blind. The first code-review prompt had 100% precision and 36% recall — it caught almost nothing precision trap. Rewriting it with explicit generalised criteria took it to 95% accuracy and 92% recall the fix, from a starting point of 64% the baseline.
  • Measure alignment per criterion, not in aggregate. In one walkthrough, product-knowledge labels matched humans 100% of the time while tone matched only once — same judge, wildly different reliability per-criterion match rate.
  • Do not report raw agreement. Two raters guessing at random already agree 50% of the time chance baseline, so use chance-corrected metrics — Cohen's kappa for two raters, Fleiss' kappa or Krippendorff's alpha for more kappa.
  • For numeric graders, mean absolute error against the human marks is the alignment metric: an MAE of 2.3 means the judge deviates from the human by ±2.3 marks on average, and you iterate the model, system prompt or rubric to drive it down MAE.
  • Close the gap with a meta-prompt: hand a powerful model the inputs, outputs, human critiques, the judge's current outcomes and the current judge prompt, and ask it to rewrite the prompt to maximise agreement meta-prompting.
  • Automated optimisation works but is not magic. GEPA lifted validation accuracy from 69% to 74% and dropped the judge's bias toward "compliant" from 98% to 64% GEPA delta — still far from the ~95% you would want to call it human-aligned still short, at roughly $200–300 of tokens for a small-scale run cost. And a prompt optimiser that makes the prompt longer and more rule-laden is not automatically better — check its output by hand check the optimiser.
  • Always test a fancy method from a paper against the simple baseline on your own labels baseline first.
  • Where this can land: Netflix reached an 85% agreement rate with expert creative writers on an extremely subjective task 85% on subjective work.
07

Gate on it — with a threshold you chose from costs

A score becomes a release signal only when the instrument has a documented error profile and a maintenance policy.

  • Report the confusion matrix. A worked example on 200 held-out responses (40 unsupported, 160 supported) where the judge catches 34 and wrongly flags 16 gives TPR 0.85, TNR 0.90, balanced accuracy 0.875 worked matrix.
  • Pick the threshold from documented costs of false negatives versus false positives, not from observed accuracy — for a safety floor, first retain only thresholds with TPR ≥ 0.90, then minimise cost among those cost-weighted threshold.
  • Never ship the judge's observed positive rate as the true rate. Correct it with sensitivity and specificity: a 78% judge positive rate with sensitivity 0.90 and specificity 0.80 corrects to roughly 83% prevalence correction.
  • And know when correction is impossible: at sensitivity and specificity near 0.50 the correction denominator collapses, and bootstrap resampling cannot repair an uninformative instrument uninformative.
  • Compare variants with paired, case-level differences and a paired bootstrap interval — resample the differences between paired cases, not A and B independently paired bootstrap.
  • Predeclare the primary metric, the evaluation set and the minimum worthwhile improvement before you run the comparison; keep the full ledger including discarded trials predeclare.
  • Run each case multiple times — five is a starting protocol — to separate difficulty (consistent failure) from instability (alternating pass/fail on identical runs) five runs. With an 0.8 single-run success rate over five attempts, "at least one passes" is 0.99968 while "all five pass" is 0.32768 — a retryable product cares about the first number, an unattended workflow about the second pass@1 vs pass-all.
  • Repeat runs are also a discovery tool: run the same eval 10, 20, 50 times over a subset to find the examples the judge is unstable on, and turn those into clearer criteria instability hunt. Raising temperature deliberately amplifies the signal while you probe probe with heat.
  • Ship the evaluator like production code — package boundary, tests, version, cost record, validation results, maintenance policy. Without those artefacts, a score is an observation, not a dependable release signal evaluator as code.
  • Define maintenance triggers in advance — excessive disagreement with human review, a new high-risk failure mode, changed provider behaviour, a score-distribution shift, rising cost, repeated evaluator errors — and never silently retune thresholds to preserve a green dashboard triggers.
  • Then roll out behind a real experiment: dogfood internally and launch as an A/B test on a small slice (~10%, or ~1% at large companies) rather than straight to full production A/B first. Decide in advance how you break the tie when the eval says good and the business metric goes down tie-break policy.
  • Run two loops, not one: one improving the application from eval signal, a second annotating eval failures to improve the eval prompt template itself two virtuous cycles.

Scoring: what you ask the judge to output

The output format is not cosmetic. It determines how consistently a human can produce ground truth, how consistently the model can reproduce it, and whether a disagreement is interpretable.

Binary over Likert

Move away from 1–5 scores and percentages toward binary pass/fail; even binary is hard to calibrate, and two human annotators often disagree on a 1–5 score anyway go binary. The academic consensus agrees: binary is easier for both the judge and the human raters to apply consistently binary preferred.

Word labels over numbers

Numeric ratings don't work well for LLM judges; use a small set of discrete word labels — yes/no, positive/negative/neutral word labels. If your evaluation can be answered with yes/no, that is a sign your specificity is right.

Pairwise beats scoring in isolation

Judges scoring a single answer tend to be too optimistic and agree with whatever they're shown too agreeable, so ask "left or right — which one is better?" like an eye exam eye exam. Pairwise gives the model a baseline and a clearer benchmark of quality comparison. Pairwise evals are a sports tournament; direct scoring is Olympic gymnastics two formats. What it buys in discrimination it gives back in order sensitivity: the judge can pick response A purely because A was mentioned first position bias. Control for that before you believe a win rate — next card.

Pairwise's own bias: position

Position bias is the tax on the format: judges "always favor one or the other, the first or the second, depending on the ordering" order, not content. Four controls, cheapest first. Randomise which response appears first, and allow ties so the judge is never forced to invent a winner between two equally good answers randomise orderallow ties. Run both orderings — ask A vs B, then B vs A, and take the majority across the two runs; if the two disagree, you do not have a verdict swap and vote. Keep the swap paired: hold rubric, evidence, decoding settings and whitespace fixed so the only thing that changed is position — a new seed or altered context makes the diagnosis ambiguous, and a flipped verdict then means neither answer has demonstrated superiority a flip is not a winner. Predeclare the aggregation rule: a production comparison either aggregates both orders or excludes and separately reports unresolved reversals under a documented rule documented rule. Anonymise candidates as response_A / response_B while you are at it, and randomise their order during the experiment anonymous pairs. If you train a judge rather than prompt one, the training-time mitigation is shuffling candidate positions intentionally so the model cannot overfit to "first" train-time shuffle. The flip rate belongs on the model card as a measured number, not an assumption on the card.

ELO when you have more than two variants

Treat each system variant as a chess player and apply ELO to the pairwise outcomes for a global ranking ELO. The open-sourced ragelo toolkit scores retrieved-hit relevance, feeds that plus both answers to a pairwise judge, then runs ELO to rank agents ragelo. Do not average per-query ELO — that is not what ELO means don't average.

Pointwise, pairwise, listwise

Judge design inherits the pointwise/pairwise/listwise learning-to-rank paradigms from IR LTR roots. Pointwise is cheap and scalable but its absolute scores can be inconsistent or miscalibrated pointwise; pairwise correlates better with human judgment at higher comparison cost pairwise; listwise ranks whole lists with NDCG but is the most expensive and hardest to scale listwise.

Score atomic facts, not whole passages

Decompose the output into a list of atomic facts with one LLM call, then check each atomic facts, weighting by importance if you need partial credit — a 4-fact passage with 2 correct facts scored 6 out of 10 partial credit. The same move fixes relevance: break the response into facts and score each fact's relevance, then aggregate into a ratio, rather than asking for a subjective holistic label fact-level relevance.

Never collapse to one number

Averaging metrics into a single weighted quality score loses the diagnostic signal about where the app is failing; plot several metrics together instead keep them apart. Keep dimensions separate and independently owned; a composite decision may be useful but must be built visibly from component results with an explicit policy visible composition. A scorer should report check-level evidence — name, expected, observed, pass/fail — rather than an aggregate like 0.75 check-level evidence.

Longer reasoning, then a short summary

Netflix asked for a reasoning trace first because the reasoning becomes context for the score reason first; pushing the trace from a couple of sentences to four or five paragraphs improved judge accuracy by about 7% absolute, with diminishing returns at the longest setting +7%. Then summarise the long trace into ~2 readable sentences — the summarisation step itself improved accuracy further tiered rationale.

Consensus and panels

Generating five judge outputs and taking the rounded average gave a noticeable accuracy boost, and paired best with the longer rationale that introduces the variance worth aggregating over consensus. A panel of different judge models aggregated by vote reduces individual-model bias, trading compute for reliability panel of judges.

Confidence, when the API gives it to you

For autoregressive judges the log-probability of the returned label token is a solid confidence proxy; small encoder-only models hand you a classification probability directly log-prob confidence. And remember a useful metric need not be a number at all — a label or explanation string surfaced to a human is a metric text as metric.

Validate the instrument

A judge that agrees with your labels can still be measuring the wrong thing. Two suites belong in every judge's model card: bias diagnostics and statistical reliability.

The bias suite

Bias here is not only an incorrect label — it is a systematic tendency to reward an irrelevant property. Four diagnostics belong on the card: position bias, verbosity bias, self-preference, and task-specific bias four diagnostics.

Position bias → the swap test

In pairwise judging the model can pick response A just because it was first position bias. Ask both orderings and take the majority across the two runs both orders, or simply randomise which appears first randomise. Make it a paired operation, not a fresh sample: if the verdict changes when only the positions change, that is evidence of position sensitivity paired swap. At training time, shuffling candidate order intentionally is the mitigation train-time shuffle.

Verbosity bias → matched length slices

Judges prefer longer, more elaborate responses even when they aren't more correct verbosity bias. Keep compared responses similar in length so the comparison is fair match length, and diagnose properly with matched slices — concise vs verbose carrying equivalent facts — reporting preference within each slice rather than the aggregate win rate slice it. This is not theoretical: in one dry run the judge favoured longer answers, letting verbose responses with unsupported claims beat short correct ones observed.

Self-preference → cross-family checks

A judge gives elevated scores to its own family's style and instruction conventions self-preference. Run the same fixture suite through a judge from the generator's family and one from a different family, and against non-LLM checks — agreement across families is useful evidence of stability but not proof of correctness cross-family.

Task-specific bias → strip the surface

Judges reward features that correlate with quality but aren't quality: polished greetings, disclaimers, a particular formatting style surface features. A 2024-era study of four leading judge models found none reliable across the board, including judges that flipped their verdict on formatting changes that left meaning unchanged none reliable.

Leakage → anonymise the candidates

Present candidates as anonymous response_A / response_B, randomise order, and evaluate each independently against the same context. Hidden identity removes one obvious source of preference leakage but does not eliminate the other three biases anonymise. Keep every test item, paraphrase and label rationale out of the judge's few-shot prompt and regression fixtures no leakage.

Vague rubrics → reward hacking

A judge's reliability depends entirely on how objectively its criteria and rubrics are specified; ambiguity there is a direct path to reward hacking reward hacking. And the rationale is not a defence — the judge's rationale is also model output and must be validated like the verdict rationale is not proof, coming out of the same tangled generation path as the verdict rather than a trace of real reasoning post-hoc.

The reliability suite

Bias tests tell you what the judge is measuring. These tell you how much to believe the number it produced.

Freeze a test split first

Split labelled data before touching the judge prompt, and treat the final partition as a sealed decision surface opened only after the configuration is frozen — otherwise it is no longer a clean estimate on unseen cases sealed split.

Correct for the judge's own error

The observed positive rate is not the true rate. Correct it with sensitivity and specificity — P = (Q + C − 1) / (S + C − 1) — and know that when both approach 0.50 the correction carries no information correction.

Pair your comparisons

Pairing removes baseline task-difficulty noise that independent samples leave in; predeclare a minimum improvement (e.g. 0.03) before calling a variant a release candidate pairing.

Measure the distribution, not one run

Don't check whether a single run gave the answer you wanted — run it multiple times and dig into the distribution of outcomes distribution. Re-running the same evaluation can give different results from non-determinism alone, observed frequently on 4o-mini-class models non-determinism.

Mutation-test the scorer

Remove a required key, change a number's type, empty the recommendations list, add an unallowed amenity, raise the price above the limit, change the recipient — each mutation should produce the intended failure and matching evidence, not a false pass mutation testing.

Keep a human in the loop, forever

LLM evals scale human judgment; they do not replace it, so some human-in-the-loop is always required for the best results scale, not replace. Even where a judge is not strictly required, periodically collect human ratings alongside judge scores and run a correlation analysis so you don't over-optimise against a flawed proxy periodic correlation. Human judgment remains the gold standard for fluency, naturalness and real-world usefulness gold standard.

In production: cost, guardrails and agents

Offline calibration is the easy half. The hard half is what the judge costs, where it sits in the request path, which way it fails, and whether a fixed rubric can describe an agent at all.

Those four questions are positional — they are answered by where the judge sits, not by how good its prompt is. Every placement in the cards below is drawn in Draw the system: the attachment points on an agent (input, retrieval, tool calls, output, trajectory), the three request-path topologies and what each one does to p95, the instrument ladder, and the calibration loop that feeds them.

Price it at production volume

Judge checks run in seconds, not milliseconds, and you pay per check forever — stacking multiple judges scales expensively fast seconds, not ms. Price at actual production call volume, not demo volume: a chatbot check runs on every message and may need several checks per message real volume. Even a toy harness makes three paid calls per test question three calls.

Budget the tail, not the mean

When a check sits in the user's critical path, consistency matters as much as average latency — measure the 95th percentile, because one person waiting two minutes ruins the experience even if most get sub-five-second responses p95.

Evals are not guardrails

Guardrails act at inference time to prevent problems; evals are a check after the fact, good for offline evaluation and continuous monitoring — an eval can detect a prompt injection but not prevent it the distinction. Practically, only safety and jailbreak checks are cheap enough to run in the moment; relevance, hallucination and completeness run post-hoc when to check. Fire the guardrail on the query in parallel with generation rather than checking the response afterwards, so you don't add a latency layer for the 95% of users who aren't misusing the product run in parallel.

Decide which way you fail

Nondeterministic guardrails tend to fail toward leniency — letting a bad output through rather than holding it back, which is the more dangerous direction fail lenient. Overblocking costs an annoyed customer and a contact-centre call; a breach reaching a regulator costs vastly more. Decide explicitly: do you fail open or fail closed, and does that argue for two independent checks? open or closed A judge is best used as one voice among several rather than the single decision-maker one voice.

Decompose the hard question

Instead of asking one frontier judge "is this message a breach", decompose the policy into small narrow checks a model answers reliably — "did you name a specific product?" — which lets you use smaller, cheaper models and shrinks latency decompose. Push as much of the criterion as possible into deterministic checks; they are fast, reproducible and easier to debug than a global quality score deterministic first. Hard requirements need explicit code, because without it a judge will reward longer and more persuasive answers that still violate the task hard requirements.

Pick the least interpretive instrument

Choose the least interpretive tool that can answer the criterion — executable check, reference check, classifier, judge, human — and add others only when the criterion has independent parts least interpretive. The compact rule: output shape → executable check; exact identity or database state → reference check; known policy categories → classifier; borderline meaning, relevance or evidence support → a judge with reference checks and sampled human review; high-consequence ambiguity → humans plus deterministic checks selection rule. The toolbox is bigger than the judge: encoder-only classifiers, human feedback, golden datasets and code heuristics all belong in it the toolbox.

Make it survivable in an audit

Audit fitness rests on three things: did the guardrail explain why it failed, is that explanation stable over time, and is the decision stored and retrievable — could you defend a verdict pulled from eight months ago? eight months on You need to name the exact rule and condition, and store the verbatim customer message rather than a summary verbatim evidence. Keep the system model-agnostic so it survives deprecations — heavily prompt-tuned systems are the most fragile to a model swap model-agnostic. And prompt tuning has a ceiling: it moves metrics but cannot fix the black-box problem, and there will always be cases outside the expected boundaries the ceiling.

Route failures to a root-cause battery

Take the 10–20% of production cases that fail a high-level quality check and run a battery of 5–10 targeted checks on just those, to tell retrieval failure from citation failure from hallucination from a bad embedding model root-cause battery. Filter all zero-scoring failures and cluster their common topics to turn a pile of failures into a diagnosis cluster failures. Instrument every stage — query rewrite, sub-query generation, retrieval, reranking — not just the final response every stage.

Skip evaluating what's already broken

If an agent's control-flow step fails, don't evaluate everything downstream of it — those results are likely invalid anyway, and skipping saves compute conditional evals. And when a guardrail fires, the fix is almost always in the orchestration and prompt, not more guardrail tuning fix system one.

Where the corpus disagrees

Four live splits. Ignore anyone who tells you these are settled.

How big should the judge model be?

Big: use the most powerful model you can afford, not mini-tier Ebbelaar; the Stanford position is a judge with much bigger capacity than what it evaluates, so it cannot be fooled by plausible-but-wrong answers Stanford.

Small: don't use an expensive frontier model for routine evaluation — the cost defeats the purpose of automating it UpTrain. Evidently's data splits the difference: GPT-4 mini hit 95–98% accuracy once the prompt was detailed enough mid-tier works, but one step down to GPT-3.5 Turbo collapsed it until it doesn't.

Our read: the tier floor is real and task-dependent, so find it empirically on your own labels. Use the expensive model for offline prompt refinement and the cheapest model that clears your calibration bar for the online judge split the roles.

Numeric scores: broken, or fine?

Broken: numeric ratings don't work well for LLM judges — use discrete word labels Arize, and move away from 1–5 scores and percentages entirely Agenta.

Fine: the UPSC grading case study runs entirely on continuous marks with MAE against human scores and reports no reliability problem CampusX, and Bedrock's built-in metrics are all continuous 0–1 scores used directly Bedrock.

Our read: the split tracks whether a human reference already uses numbers. Exam marking has a real numeric ground truth; "quality 1–10" does not. Netflix's result is the tiebreaker for subjective criteria — switching annotators from a 1–4 Likert scale to binary was a key agreement win Netflix.

Score one answer, or compare two?

Compare: single-answer judges are too optimistic and agree with whatever they're shown, so use pairwise plus ELO for complex RAG and agent systems OpenSearch. Even when absolute scores disagree with humans, sorting variants worst-to-best shows real correlation with expert rankings rank, don't score.

Score: a single-answer binary judge reached 95–98% accuracy against human labels through iterative prompt refinement, no pairwise needed Evidently.

Our read: pairwise for choosing between variants, pointwise for monitoring one system in production — you cannot rank a live trace against anything. Note that pairwise output doubles as synthetic preference data for reward-model training bonus use.

Can a fixed rubric evaluate an agent?

Yes: trajectory evaluation is just another application of LLM-as-judge — grade against a reference path, or without one by matching hit nodes to the expected process Arize/Ngo.

No: classical fixed-rubric judging could not catch the failures Arize's own agent produced — forgetting context, getting stuck in loops, generating different dynamic UI every turn Arize/Dhinakaran — so a third eval category is needed alongside deterministic checks and LLM-as-judge agent-as-judge.

Our read: both camps are describing the same fix at different granularity — decompose the judgment until each piece is answerable. Netflix's factuality agents did exactly that and produced their biggest gain decomposition wins.
