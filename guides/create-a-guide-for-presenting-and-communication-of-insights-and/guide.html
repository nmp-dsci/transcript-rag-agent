A field guide from the transcript corpus

Lead with the answer. Then earn it.

A rubric and a method for communicating insights to stakeholders — distilled from ex-McKinsey, ex-BCG and ex-Bain consultants breaking down real client decks, plus solutions architects, data analysts and AI practitioners who have to make findings land in a room, a report, or a running app.

What strong actually means

A strong presentation is a decision delivered, not a body of work displayed. The corpus is unanimous on the mechanism: school trains you to lay a trail of breadcrumbs and put the conclusion at the end; business inverts it, because the person you are presenting to has no time for breadcrumbs and wants the destination. One ex-McKinsey consultant calls the flip the single biggest upgrade most professionals ever make, and a BCG walkthrough describes the same inversion as the structural difference between a university paper and a consulting deck: key message first, arguments after.

Everything else in this guide is machinery for that one move. Structure it as a pyramid — the tip is your answer, your recommendation, your so-what, the base is the evidence — a shape Barbara Minto invented at McKinsey and every top firm now teaches as its core executive-communication technique. Support it with exactly three arguments, because three is the minimum for pattern recognition and roughly the maximum working memory holds without overload. Make those three mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive so nobody can poke a hole in the set.

And treat the packaging as real work. The storyline — not the design — is the most important part of the deck, and presenting findings is a skill in itself, separate from producing them. Teams burn the project on research and analysis and then skimp on synthesis because they already know the answer — which is exactly the part the executive is buying.

“This shift from building up to an answer to leading with an answer is the single biggest communication upgrade most professionals need to make today.”

Decide before you build

Know the goal — alignment, education, or problem-solving — and what you need the room to think, feel and do. Then clarify that goal before you open slide software.

Write the argument, not the artefact

Draft the storyline in a document. Jumping straight into PowerPoint after analysis is the biggest mistake people make, because contradictions between slide 3 and slide 19 hide in a deck.

Make every page a claim

Each page carries one full-sentence takeaway, and reading only those takeaways should give the whole argument. Nothing in a title may sit unjustified by data below it.

The rubric: eight dimensions, sixteen points

Use this to review a deck, a report, a screen, or a dashboard before it ships. Score each dimension 0 — absent · 1 — partial · 2 — strong. Below 12, send it back. The dimensions and their tests come from the corpus; the 0–2 scale and the 12-point bar are an editorial construction of this guide, not something any of the eighteen talks specify. One of the reviewers in this corpus is blunt that there is no such thing as the law of slide presentations — treat this as a calibrated default you override with reasons, not scripture.

1 · Message

Can a reader state your single takeaway before you say a word?

  • Strong: the analysis was done first, so you know the message before the page exists.
  • Strong: the headline number is in the title — “sales down 12%”, not “Sales performance”.
  • Weak: the title names the topic but takes no position — descriptive, not a point of view.
  • Weak: a bare topic header, which is the clearest thing missing when a page reads as noise.

2 · Structure

Answer first, then three non-overlapping reasons, then evidence.

  • Strong: the recommendation lands in the first minute — start with the answer first, evidence on request.
  • Strong: core idea, then two or three supporting reasons, then evidence one level down.
  • Strong: long decks open with an executive summary of every main finding, so leadership can skip the meeting and still know the content.
  • Weak: chronological build-up — question, method, findings, then a recommendation at the end. People have short attention spans; they will be gone.

3 · Evidence

Every claim traceable to data, and the data traceable to a source.

  • Strong: claims sit on visible numbers — slides full of data read as credible even before commentary.
  • Strong: a source note says how the numbers were produced; its absence is what one reviewer wanted fixed — notes on how they got to these numbers.
  • Strong: sources cited on the page itself; the instruction is flat — make sure you're citing your sources.
  • Weak: scope undefined, so the number can't be read at all — what industry exactly are you talking about here.

4 · Comparison

No number appears alone. Every figure carries a reference point.

  • Strong: the rule is absolute — never share a single data point in isolation.
  • Strong: one of three comparisons is present: time frame, same-category benchmark, or category average.
  • Strong: the comparison creates the story — a 63% increase against a 12% industry increase.
  • Weak: cohorts that aren't alike. New customers versus existing customers isn't a finding, it's trust already built — compare apples to apples.

5 · The page

One goal per page, one grid, one accent colour, one body font size.

  • Strong: you can name the page's single job before adding data — know your main goal of that slide.
  • Strong: attention is directed, not hunted for: don't make the audience go searching.
  • Strong: body type is uniform — everything except title and footnotes at one size — with hierarchy carried by size, not chrome.
  • Weak: five type sizes on one page — 28, then 24, then 20, then 18, then 12.

6 · Audience fit

Built for the specific people in the room, at their context level.

  • Strong: you named the audience before building — technical readers want method, business readers don't — think about who your target audience is.
  • Strong: you opened with context, because the golden rule is always assume zero context even after three weeks inside the project.
  • Strong: you mapped their fears, their concerns, and their goals and addressed them before they were raised.
  • Weak: insider conventions nobody outside your function reads — the banking “football field” chart, where nobody understands this outside of banking.

7 · Action

The audience leaves knowing what happens next and who owns it.

  • Strong: the deck closes on implications — make it a habit to always close with a core message and an implication.
  • Strong: recommendations are enumerated — the answer section culminates in a final slide with three key recommendations.
  • Strong: any written update ends with the next action, so the reader doesn't re-derive it.
  • Weak: impact claimed in model or method terms instead of faster task completion and lower operational costs.

8 · Honesty

Limits, risks and unknowns are stated by you, not discovered by them.

  • Strong: you say what you don't know. Evaluators reward people who know their limits and don't just answer.
  • Strong: the framed version buys credibility rather than losing it — this is something I may not be able to answer at this time.
  • Strong: risk is surfaced explicitly, including the unglamorous kind: lack of explainability is itself a business risk.
  • Weak: confidence you can't defend. Bluffing is precisely what breaks trust — know when to stop.

The pipeline: analysis to decision in eight stages

Run it in order. Every stage before stage six happens in a document or a notebook — not in slide software, not in Figma, not in a dashboard builder.

01

Set goal, audience, approach

Most people know their topic. Knowing the goal is knowing what the other person must think, feel and do when you finish — and without it you ramble, overshare, or underexplain.

  • Name the goal as one of three buckets: alignment, education, or problem-solving. Alignment means you need a yes; education means you're levelling someone up; problem-solving means you decide together.
  • State it out loud to yourself the way the worked example does — “my goal in this meeting is alignment” — then filter every page and every answer through it.
  • Profile the audience by role. A CEO is time-poor and outcome-driven and gets the answer first; a junior analyst needs background and examples.
  • Assume they have none of your context — they've been in forty meetings — so always assume zero context and open with a short setup.
  • Frame to avoid manufacturing enemies. “Our product isn't working” attacks the head of product; “directing resources to the highest impact opportunities” carries the same message collaboratively.
  • For a customer or sales-adjacent readout, find the person who signs: authority is the person who is going to sign off, and their absence from early calls is a yellow flag.
02

Ask a question the data can answer

Opening a dashboard without a question is how you end up presenting a pattern that isn't there.

  • Set the question before you open anything: a goal or a question that you need to answer. Otherwise — if you torture data long enough it will confess to anything.
  • Make it narrow enough to name its own data source: “what were our three top-selling products in April?”
  • Then layer a comparison to turn a fact into an insight: “why is Google Ads conversion rate down 5% from the previous month?”
  • Decompose before you diagnose. A revenue move is always traffic, conversion rate, or average order value, and you start from the beginning of the user journey, not the end of the funnel.
  • Segment until the cause is named. Checkout completion at 45% became actionable only when a country split showed Canadian shipping running $50 to $80, revealed at the payment step.
03

Compare, then find the difference

An insight is a difference you can explain. A number without a reference point is not an insight; it's a reading.

  • “3% conversion rate” means nothing alone — you always have to have data to compare with.
  • Compare like with like, then read the gap: those differences are the insights. Your job from there is explaining why the difference exists.
  • A prior-year figure next to today's does the work for you: 52% optimistic and 30% concerned becomes a 17-point jump in optimism.
  • Pattern recognition is trainable, not innate — spotting patterns comes with practice and with domain depth.
04

Synthesise to one so-what

This is the step everyone underfunds and the only step the executive is paying for.

  • Do the distillation before the design. Pages fail because the author skipped it — collect the data, do the analysis, and then you know the message.
  • Understand what this stage is and isn't: storylining is packaging the conclusion you already have, not more analysis.
  • Pick a sequencing for your three points — deductive for analytical audiences, chronological for a roadmap, or comparative where you start with your most important point so it lands before anyone drifts.
  • Check the set is MECE. If someone can say “what about this?”, the argument falls apart.
05

Storyline in a document

Word before PowerPoint. A page of prose exposes logic errors that thirty slides conceal, and it is cheap to rewrite.

  • Draft on one page, because a storyline is easier to read on a single page than spread across a deck.
  • Expect several passes — writing a storyline is an iterative process of draft, manager feedback, redraft — and that loop is far faster in text.
  • Use dot-dash: each dot is a key statement and the dashes are the supporting data that justifies it. In the King County deck, the dot “homelessness continues to increase” is carried by dashes showing the population grew from 9,000 to 11,200.
  • Choose the arc. Pyramid (answer first) is the default; for data-heavy material or a sceptical room, use situation, complication, resolution so the recommendation feels earned.
  • Open the situation on agreed ground — the situation component should not be surprising — then pivot. In real decks the complication is usually findable by scanning for “but” or “however”.
  • The question in SCQA stays implicit; it's just what should we do about the problem, and the resolution is the blueprint for solving it.
  • Finish the storyline before you open slide software: changes made later mean rebuilding slides.
06

Write every title before you build anything

The titles are the deck. Build them as a list, read the list aloud, and only then make pages.

  • Write them all up front: the title of every single slide before you've made any slides. Treat the first version as a hypothesis that you expect the data to revise.
  • Each title is a full sentence, not a label — one very clear sentence summarising the key message. A real BCG example: “French companies are lagging behind in leveraging AI.”
  • Bain does the same thing — the title written out in a full sentence — and then the body supplies the proof, highest-level idea first.
  • Keep it to one line that prioritises; one line, not too long, clearly stating the takeaway beats listing everything you found.
  • Run both flow checks: horizontally the titles alone tell the argument, and vertically the dots become action titles and the dashes become the charts beneath them.
  • Check adjacency. There should never be a moment where the reader wonders why this page follows the last — each slide should build on the last one. Where you need to set up a set of topics, use a framework page that lays out all the main buckets, then deep-dive one bucket per page after it.
07

Build the page

Design is subtraction. A live audience is listening, reading and forming a response at once, so assume attention is scarce even in a smart room.

  • Budget for overload: they are trying to listen to the speaker and read the words and numbers simultaneously. A page exists to reduce load, not add a layer of it.
  • Lay out on a grid — think in thirds, chart across two-thirds and commentary in the last third, or halves and quarters. Inside the grid, think in tables: every chart is also rows and columns.
  • Pick the chart for the comparison you're making: clustered bar to compare subcategories, line for trend over time, and — against the usual advice — a table when you need precise values.
  • Remember the chart type is itself the message. Swap Bain's waterfall for a stacked column and the focus becomes the height of each column — total size, not source of growth. Ask of every rebuild: can you still see the insight?
  • Colour carries meaning or it goes. Use colour with intention; the eye reaches it before the explanation. Green/yes and red/no work because green is go and red is stop. On a data page, one accent plus black and grey is usually all you need.
  • Add the “why”, not just the “what”. Bain's key-insights callout works because it is helping you understand why it's growing the way it is, and a customer quote next to the chart brings a personal element to the data.
  • Deword ruthlessly: say as much as possible with as few words as possible. “The value of this information depends on the trust within the network” becomes “Information value depends on network trust.”
  • Push detail down, not out. Keep the focal metric on the page and add the exhaustive tables to an appendix for the data-minded. Less is always more on a slide.
08

Deliver, then prove it moved

The readout isn't the finish line. Slides are a tool you should be able to work without, and the outcome is what gets judged.

  • Open with a recap before you ask anything, especially of someone running several workstreams — first open with a recap — and explain things simply rather than in your project's private shorthand.
  • Make it two-way. After every slide or section, create ways for them to chime in, with a line as plain as “want to pause here now in case anyone has any questions.”
  • Ask one question at a time; a batch of three or four is taxing to process.
  • Treat the deck as secondary — slides are really just a tool — and remember the customer doesn't care how much effort it took; they judge the outcome.
  • Quantify what you claimed. Use A/B tests or pilot rollouts to quantify benefits instead of asserting them, and lead with the one hard number — the RAG rollout in this corpus reports a 60% reduction in documentation-retrieval time.
  • Build trust in increments rather than one big reveal: start with small pilots where you build trust and learn fast.

The room: preparation, objections, limits

Delivery in this corpus is not about charisma. The difference between clear and muddled is the amount of preparation someone put into the words — which is why the mentor's line is fail to prepare, prepare to fail. And you cannot acquire it passively; you can't be an effective presenter by just watching videos.

Ask, don't pitch

  • The fastest way to win a room is to let it do most of the talking and feed you the ideas you then respond to.
  • Aim for the shift where the audience narrates their own problem back and you probe — them doing a lot of the storytelling.
  • Overpreparing facts is a trap: presenters who do it retreat into that comfort zone under pressure, exactly when a mixed room needs them not to.
  • Success is mutual: someone who takes control of the situation so the audience got value and you learned what you needed.

Give every segment a hook

  • Build the narrative like a Disney film — content for the technical people and separate themes the business people relate to — so nobody switches off.
  • Explain complexity in plain terms, using fewer words and less jargon, so the audience gets the value without being experts.
  • Blend the registers until the audience can't see the join between the technical and the business conversation.
  • Always connect the method to the need: the top skill is explaining your technical approach and reasoning against business requirements, and deep questions probe why the technology exists, not tool trivia.

Handle the “no”

  • An objection is an opportunity — dig for the fear or perceived risk behind it rather than defending.
  • Reframe rather than hide a limitation: if you can't fix it, feature it.
  • Read the pushback as a start, not a failure: when you hear the no is when the negotiation starts.
  • Respect emotional ownership of what you're proposing to replace — we sell technology but we sell change, and someone spent two years building the thing you're critiquing.
  • Learn to spot objections planted by competitors, and treat them as intelligence.
  • Take the lifeline. When you're off track the room will often throw a steering question — use it instead of digging in.

Find the real problem first

  • What they report is a symptom: the problem is rarely ever the problem.
  • The worked case: a “dashboard latency” complaint whose actual goal was sales and conversion, not latency.
  • Never flatly contradict the framing — “I would never tell you you're wrong” — validate it while steering to the underlying goal.
  • Quantify the pain at several levels — time on manual work, frequency of costly mistakes, competitive risk — and build urgency by asking what happens if you don't do this.
  • Borrow credibility where you can: showing you have solved this shape of problem before says we've done this before, we can do it again.

Know your limits out loud

  • Have the sentence ready before you need it: “this is not an area I know about, let me get back to you.”
  • Draw the architecture even when nobody asked, purely to force clarity in your own mind — and show it, because the more they understand, the more favourably they judge you.
  • Anchor on one specialisation you can go deep on; it's worth thinking about a particular spike rather than claiming breadth everywhere.
  • Calibrate by asking, not guessing: ask your peers about your communication, and practise by writing decisions down so someone with zero context can follow the reasoning.
  • Watch the delivery habits you can't hear yourself doing — even strong speakers flag their arms and their “you knows” on review.

Plan the window

  • A discovery or readout slot is short and fixed — roughly 35 to 40 minutes — so plan its use rather than winging it.
  • You are rarely solo; a technical presenter typically works with an account executive, so agree who steers what.
  • When you need backup, be specific: be good at explaining exactly what help you need.
  • Carrying yourself in front of senior leadership has a name and it is assessed — executive presence, a term borrowed from consulting.

One calibration on how much technical depth to lead with: in a pre-sales or mixed-stakeholder setting, the soft-skill layer is the differentiator, and fewer than half of technically excellent people can do it. Technical depth is necessary; the persistent myth is that it is sufficient — that presenters mainly need to be very technical.

When the deck is an app or a dashboard

No talk in this corpus is about dashboard design — that is the largest gap in the evidence, and it is stated plainly in Gaps. What the corpus does cover, directly and repeatedly, is the two things that decide whether a screen communicates: whether each view states a conclusion, and whether the numbers on it can be traced. The transfers below are marked for what they are.

Every view needs an action title

  • A dashboard page with a chart and no takeaway is the topic-header failure in another medium: it is descriptive with no point of view. Write the header as the finding, with the number in it — “sales down 12%”.
  • One goal per screen, exactly as one goal per slide: know the main goal and cut every tile that doesn't serve it.
  • Drill-downs are your appendix. Put the focal metric up front and push the nitty-gritty tables behind it for the data-minded.
  • This one is a direct warning about screens you send rather than narrate: a minimal, visual-only page needs descriptive text under each headline if the people reading it weren't in the room.

Traceability is the trust mechanism

  • Show the provenance beside the output: good systems present citations or links to the original source documents.
  • Being able to show which documents produced an answer is what makes an AI-derived insight verifiable to a business audience rather than a matter of faith.
  • Structure synthesised findings as a report that highlights the flagged sections with explanations and citations back to source.
  • Give the reviewer a risk read so they know how hard to look — an explicit risk assessment tells me how much I need to actually look into it. Attach evidence, not assertions: hand over screenshots rather than asking the reader to reproduce.

Report quality, not uptime

  • Ongoing reporting should carry relevance and hallucination rates alongside cost and alerting — not just whether the service was up.
  • A metric on a review screen doesn't have to be a number: a text or label that helps you understand something counts, and a judge's free-text reasoning is often the most useful column.
  • Keep a human path into the raw rows. Eyeballing individual results caught a model gaming retrieval with quoted exact-phrase queries that the scored dashboard did not surface.
  • Don't trust aggregate scores alone: single-answer LLM judges are too optimistic, and quality monitoring should combine automated evaluation with human review.
  • Budget realistically for the human half: handing 50 to 100 queries to an expert typically returns one or two before they say they're too busy.
  • Build the feedback loop into the surface — thumbs up, thumbs down, comments — so context accrues instead of being lost.

Demo the thing, cheaply, early

  • Render the real candidates instead of describing them. Generating a page of options so we can choose the best one converts a debate into a decision.
  • A clickable prototype you can deploy and share with your organisation gets direction agreed before you spend on fidelity.
  • But reconnect it to the real design system before executives see it, or the verdict is “this is very off-brand” regardless of the thinking underneath.
  • Set the expectation gap explicitly. Proofs of concept showcase the wow factor; production meets complexity, limits and cost the demo audience never saw.
  • Ship the smallest version that wins. Choose the smallest scope that produces a net increase — and be honest that the advice is build a rough first version and iterate.
  • Chasing the symptom forever is a trap: latency is a bit of a swamp, where solving it once just raises the next ask.

Documentation and written updates

  • For anything read rather than presented, consistency is the priority — the same structure every time, or some readers get a different shape of information than others.
  • Get consistency from a saved spec, not from prompting afresh: define the types of information that must be included and reuse it.
  • Structure written status the same way: recap first, plain language, one question, end with the next action.
  • You still have to own the reasoning. A generated system can't be defended by someone who didn't decide it — asked whether AI can build a design system end to end, the answer is no, it cannot.

Claiming impact in writing

  • Use the formula for every claim you make about your own work: I accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z — result, metric, method.
  • Never assume the reader infers significance. If you don't tell them why what you did mattered, they won't work it out, and they decide whether to keep reading in five to seven seconds.
  • When exact figures are confidential, use percentages or multipliers, or an order of magnitude like “millions of rows” — never omit scale.
  • Write the summary last, after the evidence exists: write that part last, and treat it as a thesis the rest must prove.
  • Tailor to the stated requirements — it should be like taking an open-book quiz — and let repetition tell you what to lead with, because repetition matters.
  • Check the fit mechanically before you send: paste the document and the audience's stated requirements into a model and compare the two. In the worked case, iterating on the score moved it from about 63% to 83%.

Where the corpus disagrees

Four live disagreements. Each is a real judgement call, not a defect in the evidence — and the resolution depends on your room.

Lead with the answer, or build to it?

The pyramid principle says the answer goes first, always — start with the answer first. The same talk then says that for data-heavy presentations or a sceptical audience you should use situation, complication, resolution and hold the recommendation until it feels earned. Call: pyramid by default; SCR when the audience has to be moved off a position they already hold. Pairing them is explicitly endorsed as making a message logically bulletproof and emotionally engaging.

Finish before you show, or show rough and iterate?

The storylining discipline says settle everything in the document, because later changes mean rebuilding slides. The forward-deployed engineer says over-polishing before showing work loses deals, so build a rough first version and iterate. Call: they're about different objects. Finalise the argument before you build; ship the artefact rough and let the customer shape it.

How many colours can a data page carry?

One review argues a six-colour McKinsey chart actually lowers effort, because matching hues let a viewer group series instinctively and cognitive load is reduced compared to black and white. Another, on a BCG page, wanted a second green replaced with a more decent grey. Call: colours that encode a grouping earn their place; colours that merely decorate or add a shade do not. Highlight colours should stay decent, not aggressive.

How technical should you get?

In a technical assessment, interviewers deliberately probe deeper as seniority rises, rewarding demonstrated depth on why the technologies exist. In the situational interview at the same company, leaning on technical depth with a mixed audience is marked down; the job there is discovery, and you should let them do most of the talking. Call: depth is what you prove when asked; it is not what you lead with. And note the parallel warning from consulting — a page banking readers expect can be one that would never go into a consulting main deck.

What this corpus does not answer

Honest gaps. Where you need these, go somewhere else — do not treat the guide above as covering them.

  • Dashboard and app design. No talk here covers laying out a dashboard, designing a report screen, or choosing charts for an interactive surface. Everything in Apps & dashboards is slide and document advice transferred by argument, not by evidence.
  • Live delivery mechanics. Nothing on pacing, vocal tone, body language, or how to open and close a live presentation. The closest the corpus gets is one presenter noticing his own filler words on replay.
  • Remote and asynchronous presenting. No screen-share etiquette, no camera framing, no guidance on a recorded walkthrough or a written memo replacing a live readout.
  • Hostile Q&A over a chart. Objection handling here is framed around sales conversations. There is no worked example of defending a forecast or a contested number under live pushback.
  • Statistical rigour. Nothing on sample size, significance, or confidence — so nothing on deciding when a difference is a real insight rather than noise, and nothing on presenting uncertainty to non-technical stakeholders.
  • Accessibility. Every source leans on colour coding as a core technique; none mention colourblind-safe palettes or any other accessibility consideration.
  • Deck length and slide budgets. No numbers on how many slides a 30-minute meeting should carry, how many words are too many for a page, or how long a dewording pass should take.
  • Proof that any of this works. No win rates, time-to-approval, or lift metrics comparing a structured deck against an unstructured one. The case for SCR and the pyramid principle in this corpus is practitioner authority, not measurement.
  • Pre-meeting iteration. Plenty on inviting input during the meeting, nothing on how to gather and fold in stakeholder feedback on a draft before it.
  • Did it change anything? No source addresses tracking whether a presentation actually moved a decision or a behaviour afterwards — the one measurement that would close the loop on everything above.
