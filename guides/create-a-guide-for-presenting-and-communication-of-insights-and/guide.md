# The Executive Readout

**Lead with the answer. Then earn it.**

A rubric and a method for communicating insights to stakeholders — distilled from ex-McKinsey, ex-BCG and ex-Bain consultants breaking down real client decks, plus solutions architects, data analysts and AI practitioners who have to make findings land in a room, a report, or a running app.

## What strong actually means

A strong presentation is a **decision delivered**, not a body of work displayed. The corpus is unanimous on the mechanism: school trains you to lay a trail of breadcrumbs and put the conclusion at the end; business inverts it, because the person you are presenting to has no time for breadcrumbs and wants the destination. One ex-McKinsey consultant calls the flip the single biggest upgrade most professionals ever make [c7_riQh50UE@2], and a BCG walkthrough describes the same inversion as the structural difference between a university paper and a consulting deck: key message first, arguments after [nAlR78kKSTs@2].

Everything else in this guide is machinery for that one move. Structure it as a pyramid — the tip is your answer, your recommendation, your so-what [c7_riQh50UE@10], the base is the evidence — a shape Barbara Minto invented at McKinsey and every top firm now teaches as its core executive-communication technique [jUFFkh5YSU8@11]. Support it with exactly three arguments, because three is the minimum for pattern recognition and roughly the maximum working memory holds without overload [c7_riQh50UE@12]. Make those three mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive [c7_riQh50UE@15] so nobody can poke a hole in the set.

And treat the packaging as real work. The storyline — not the design — is the most important part of the deck [CY1Y367KEko@0], and presenting findings is a skill in itself [3bVc_-m_fMg@15], separate from producing them. Teams burn the project on research and analysis and then skimp on synthesis because they already know the answer [nuosoMyf0eA@5] — which is exactly the part the executive is buying.

> "This shift from building up to an answer to leading with an answer is the single biggest communication upgrade most professionals need to make today." [c7_riQh50UE@2]

**Three moves the corpus converges on:**

1. **Decide before you build.** Know the goal — alignment, education, or problem-solving [c7_riQh50UE@4] — and what you need the room to think, feel and do [c7_riQh50UE@3]. Clarify that goal before you open slide software [jUFFkh5YSU8@2].
2. **Write the argument, not the artefact.** Draft the storyline in a document. Jumping straight into PowerPoint after analysis is the biggest mistake people make [CY1Y367KEko@5], because contradictions between slide 3 and slide 19 hide in a deck [CY1Y367KEko@6].
3. **Make every page a claim.** Each page carries one full-sentence takeaway, and reading only those takeaways should give the whole argument [CY1Y367KEko@9]. Nothing in a title may sit unjustified by data below it [CY1Y367KEko@9].

## The rubric: eight dimensions, sixteen points

Use this to review a deck, a report, a screen, or a dashboard before it ships. Score each dimension **0 — absent · 1 — partial · 2 — strong**. Below 12, send it back. The dimensions and their tests come from the corpus; the 0–2 scale and the 12-point bar are an editorial construction of this guide, not something any of the eighteen talks specify. One reviewer here is blunt that there is no such thing as the law of slide presentations [YXQWfTPQoSg@14] — treat this as a calibrated default you override with reasons, not scripture.

### 1 · Message
*Can a reader state your single takeaway before you say a word?*

- **Strong:** the analysis was done first, so you know the message before the page exists [9WCPpEIIdrE@2].
- **Strong:** the headline number is in the title — "sales down 12%" [3bVc_-m_fMg@14], not "Sales performance".
- **Weak:** the title names the topic but takes no position — descriptive, not a point of view [9WCPpEIIdrE@4].
- **Weak:** a bare topic header, which is the clearest thing missing [YXQWfTPQoSg@7] when a page reads as noise.

### 2 · Structure
*Answer first, then three non-overlapping reasons, then evidence.*

- **Strong:** the recommendation lands in the first minute — start with the answer first [c7_riQh50UE@11], evidence on request.
- **Strong:** core idea, then two or three supporting reasons [jUFFkh5YSU8@11], then evidence one level down.
- **Strong:** long decks open with an executive summary of every main finding [nAlR78kKSTs@1], so leadership can skip the meeting and still know the content.
- **Weak:** chronological build-up — question, method, findings, recommendation last. People have short attention spans [jUFFkh5YSU8@12]; they will be gone.

### 3 · Evidence
*Every claim traceable to data, and the data traceable to a source.*

- **Strong:** claims sit on visible numbers — slides full of data [9WCPpEIIdrE@6] read as credible even before commentary.
- **Strong:** a source note says how the numbers were produced; its absence is what one reviewer wanted fixed [9WCPpEIIdrE@6].
- **Strong:** sources cited on the page itself — make sure you're citing your sources [jUFFkh5YSU8@8].
- **Weak:** scope undefined, so the number can't be read at all — what industry exactly are you talking about here [YXQWfTPQoSg@8].

### 4 · Comparison
*No number appears alone. Every figure carries a reference point.*

- **Strong:** the rule is absolute — never share a single data point in isolation [nuosoMyf0eA@0].
- **Strong:** one of three comparisons is present: time frame, same-category benchmark, or category average [nuosoMyf0eA@1].
- **Strong:** the comparison creates the story — a 63% increase against a 12% industry increase [nuosoMyf0eA@1].
- **Weak:** cohorts that aren't alike. New customers versus existing customers isn't a finding, it's trust already built — compare apples to apples [3bVc_-m_fMg@5].

### 5 · The page
*One goal per page, one grid, one accent colour, one body font size.*

- **Strong:** you can name the page's single job before adding data [3bVc_-m_fMg@12].
- **Strong:** attention is directed, not hunted for — don't make the audience go searching [WZuDhxS40qo@5].
- **Strong:** body type is uniform — everything except title and footnotes at one size [YXQWfTPQoSg@2] — with hierarchy carried by size, not chrome [9WCPpEIIdrE@7].
- **Weak:** five type sizes on one page — 28, then 24, then 20, then 18, then 12 [YXQWfTPQoSg@2].

### 6 · Audience fit
*Built for the specific people in the room, at their context level.*

- **Strong:** you named the audience before building — technical readers want method, business readers don't [3bVc_-m_fMg@1].
- **Strong:** you opened with context, because the golden rule is always assume zero context [c7_riQh50UE@6] even after three weeks inside the project.
- **Strong:** you mapped their fears, their concerns, and their goals [c7_riQh50UE@7] and addressed them before they were raised.
- **Weak:** insider conventions nobody outside your function reads — the banking "football field" chart, where nobody understands this outside of banking [YXQWfTPQoSg@13].

### 7 · Action
*The audience leaves knowing what happens next and who owns it.*

- **Strong:** the deck closes on implications — always close with a core message and an implication [nAlR78kKSTs@13].
- **Strong:** recommendations are enumerated — a final slide with three key recommendations [nuosoMyf0eA@3].
- **Strong:** any written update ends with the next action [Ukju3maxbEQ@18], so the reader doesn't re-derive it.
- **Weak:** impact claimed in model or method terms instead of faster task completion and lower operational costs [uaiq1HvQKoI@42].

### 8 · Honesty
*Limits, risks and unknowns are stated by you, not discovered by them.*

- **Strong:** you say what you don't know. Evaluators reward people who know their limits and don't just answer [hahPTeXJEBk@5].
- **Strong:** the framed version buys credibility rather than losing it — "this is something I may not be able to answer at this time" [hahPTeXJEBk@21].
- **Strong:** risk is surfaced explicitly, including lack of explainability [uaiq1HvQKoI@44], which is itself a business risk.
- **Weak:** confidence you can't defend. Bluffing is what breaks trust — know when to stop [hahPTeXJEBk@20].

### Scoring

| Score | Verdict | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 14–16 | Ship | Polish jitter and footnotes only. Not every slide in consulting is perfect [nAlR78kKSTs@11] as long as the key message lands. |
| 10–13 | Rewrite titles | Extract every title into a document and read them end to end — write out all of the slide titles as bullet points [CY1Y367KEko@3]. |
| 0–9 | Back to the storyline | Stop editing pages. Restart from the argument, and check you can summarize the entire storyline in three sentences [CY1Y367KEko@4]. |

## The pipeline: analysis to decision in eight stages

Run it in order. Every stage before stage six happens in a document or a notebook — not in slide software, not in Figma, not in a dashboard builder.

### 01 · Set goal, audience, approach

*Most people know their topic. Knowing the goal is knowing what the other person must think, feel and do when you finish — and without it you ramble, overshare, or underexplain.*

- Name the goal as one of three buckets: alignment, education, or problem-solving [c7_riQh50UE@4].
- State it out loud — "my goal in this meeting is alignment" [c7_riQh50UE@20] — then filter every page and every answer through it.
- Profile the audience by role. A CEO is time-poor and outcome-driven [c7_riQh50UE@5] and gets the answer first; a junior analyst needs background and examples.
- Assume they have none of your context — always assume zero context [c7_riQh50UE@6] — and open with a short setup.
- Frame to avoid manufacturing enemies. "Our product isn't working" attacks the head of product; "directing resources to the highest impact opportunities" [c7_riQh50UE@8] carries the same message collaboratively.
- For a customer readout, find the person who signs: authority is the person who is going to sign off [Z9S9oxvYUvk@8], and their absence from early calls is a yellow flag [Z9S9oxvYUvk@8].

### 02 · Ask a question the data can answer

*Opening a dashboard without a question is how you end up presenting a pattern that isn't there.*

- Set the question before you open anything: a goal or a question that you need to answer [3bVc_-m_fMg@1]. Otherwise — if you torture data long enough it will confess to anything [3bVc_-m_fMg@0].
- Make it narrow enough to name its own data source: "what were our three top-selling products in April?" [3bVc_-m_fMg@1]
- Then layer a comparison to turn a fact into an insight: "why is Google Ads conversion rate down 5% from the previous month?" [3bVc_-m_fMg@2]
- Decompose before you diagnose. A revenue move is always traffic, conversion rate, or average order value [3bVc_-m_fMg@3], and you start from the beginning of the user journey [3bVc_-m_fMg@3].
- Segment until the cause is named. Checkout completion at 45% [3bVc_-m_fMg@7] became actionable only when a country split showed Canadian shipping running $50 to $80 [3bVc_-m_fMg@8], revealed at the payment step.

### 03 · Compare, then find the difference

*An insight is a difference you can explain. A number without a reference point is not an insight; it's a reading.*

- "3% conversion rate" means nothing alone — you always have to have data to compare with [3bVc_-m_fMg@5].
- Compare like with like, then read the gap: those differences are the insights [3bVc_-m_fMg@6].
- A prior-year figure next to today's does the work: 52% optimistic and 30% concerned becomes a 17-point jump in optimism [nuosoMyf0eA@0].
- Pattern recognition is trainable — spotting patterns comes with practice [3bVc_-m_fMg@9] and with domain depth.

### 04 · Synthesise to one so-what

*This is the step everyone underfunds and the only step the executive is paying for.*

- Do the distillation before the design: collect the data, do the analysis, and then you know the message [9WCPpEIIdrE@2].
- Storylining is packaging the conclusion you already have [CY1Y367KEko@5], not more analysis.
- Pick a sequencing for your three points — deductive [c7_riQh50UE@13] for analytical audiences, chronological [c7_riQh50UE@14] for a roadmap, or comparative where you start with your most important point [c7_riQh50UE@14] so it lands before anyone drifts.
- Check the set is MECE. If someone can say "what about this?", the argument falls apart [c7_riQh50UE@15].

### 05 · Storyline in a document

*Word before PowerPoint. A page of prose exposes logic errors that thirty slides conceal, and it is cheap to rewrite.*

- Draft on one page — a storyline is easier to read on a single page [CY1Y367KEko@5] than spread across a deck.
- Expect several passes; writing a storyline is an iterative process [CY1Y367KEko@5] of draft, feedback, redraft, and that loop is far faster in text.
- Use dot-dash: each dot is a key statement and the dashes are the supporting data that justifies it [CY1Y367KEko@6]. In the King County deck, the dot "homelessness continues to increase" is carried by dashes showing the population grew from 9,000 to 11,200 [CY1Y367KEko@7].
- Choose the arc. Pyramid (answer first) is the default; for data-heavy material or a sceptical room, use situation, complication, resolution [c7_riQh50UE@16] so the recommendation feels earned.
- Open the situation on agreed ground — the situation component should not be surprising [c7_riQh50UE@17] — then pivot. In real decks the complication is usually findable by scanning for "but" or "however" [nuosoMyf0eA@2].
- The question in SCQA stays implicit; it's just what should we do about the problem [CY1Y367KEko@2], and the resolution is the blueprint for solving it [CY1Y367KEko@2].
- Finish the storyline before you open slide software: changes made later mean rebuilding slides [CY1Y367KEko@8].

### 06 · Write every title before you build anything

*The titles are the deck. Build them as a list, read the list aloud, and only then make pages.*

- Write them all up front: the title of every single slide before you've made any slides [jUFFkh5YSU8@3]. Treat the first version as a hypothesis you expect the data to revise [jUFFkh5YSU8@3].
- Each title is a full sentence, not a label — one very clear sentence summarising the key message [nAlR78kKSTs@3]. A real BCG example: "French companies are lagging behind in leveraging AI." [nAlR78kKSTs@3]
- Bain does the same — the title written out in a full sentence [WZuDhxS40qo@1] — and the body supplies the proof, highest-level idea first [WZuDhxS40qo@2].
- Keep it to one line that prioritises; one line, not too long, clearly stating [9WCPpEIIdrE@3] the takeaway beats listing everything you found.
- Run both flow checks: horizontally the titles alone tell the argument, and vertically the dots become action titles and the dashes become the charts beneath them [CY1Y367KEko@9].
- Check adjacency — each slide should build on the last one [jUFFkh5YSU8@6]. Where you need to set up a set of topics, use a framework page that lays out all the main buckets [jUFFkh5YSU8@6], then deep-dive one bucket per page.

### 07 · Build the page

*Design is subtraction. A live audience is listening, reading and forming a response at once, so assume attention is scarce even in a smart room.*

- Budget for overload: they are trying to listen to the speaker and read the words and numbers [WZuDhxS40qo@6] simultaneously. A page exists to reduce load, not add a layer of it [jUFFkh5YSU8@4].
- Lay out on a grid — think in thirds [nAlR78kKSTs@4], chart across two-thirds and commentary in the last third, or halves and quarters. Inside the grid, think in tables [nAlR78kKSTs@8]: every chart is also rows and columns.
- Pick the chart for the comparison: clustered bar to compare subcategories [nuosoMyf0eA@7], line for trend over time [nuosoMyf0eA@8], and — against the usual advice — a table when you need precise values [nuosoMyf0eA@8].
- The chart type is itself the message. Swap Bain's waterfall for a stacked column and the focus becomes the height of each column [WZuDhxS40qo@4] — total size, not source of growth. Ask of every rebuild: can you still see the insight? [WZuDhxS40qo@4]
- Colour carries meaning or it goes. Use colour with intention [nuosoMyf0eA@6]; the eye reaches it before the explanation. Green/yes and red/no work because green is go and red is stop [nuosoMyf0eA@7]. On a data page, one accent plus black and grey [WZuDhxS40qo@6] is usually all you need.
- Add the "why", not just the "what". Bain's key-insights callout works because it is helping you understand why it's growing the way it is [WZuDhxS40qo@7], and a customer quote next to the chart brings a personal element to the data [WZuDhxS40qo@7].
- Deword ruthlessly: say as much as possible with as few words as possible [YXQWfTPQoSg@9]. "The value of this information depends on the trust within the network" becomes "Information value depends on network trust." [YXQWfTPQoSg@9]
- Push detail down, not out. Keep the focal metric on the page and add the exhaustive tables to an appendix [3bVc_-m_fMg@13]. Less is always more on a slide [jUFFkh5YSU8@7].

### 08 · Deliver, then prove it moved

*The readout isn't the finish line. Slides are a tool you should be able to work without, and the outcome is what gets judged.*

- Open with a recap before you ask anything [Ukju3maxbEQ@17] and explain things simply [Ukju3maxbEQ@18] rather than in your project's private shorthand.
- Make it two-way. After every slide or section, create ways for them to chime in [jUFFkh5YSU8@15], with a line as plain as "want to pause here now in case anyone has any questions." [jUFFkh5YSU8@15]
- Ask one question at a time [Ukju3maxbEQ@18]; a batch of three or four is taxing to process.
- Treat the deck as secondary — slides are really just a tool [jUFFkh5YSU8@11] — and remember the customer doesn't care how much effort it took [Ck-LeYlAVbY@15]; they judge the outcome.
- Quantify what you claimed. Use A/B tests or pilot rollouts to quantify benefits [uaiq1HvQKoI@42] instead of asserting them, and lead with the one hard number — this corpus's RAG rollout reports a 60% reduction in documentation-retrieval time [uaiq1HvQKoI@11].
- Build trust in increments rather than one big reveal: start with small pilots where you build trust and learn fast [uaiq1HvQKoI@48].

## Delete on sight

Fifteen things the reviewers in this corpus cut from real decks, and what goes in their place. Run this pass last, when the argument is already settled.

| Delete on sight | Replace with |
|---|---|
| A topic header — "Market and Industry" | A full-sentence takeaway. The missing action title is the clearest thing missing [YXQWfTPQoSg@7]; it is a fundamental piece of every slide presentation [YXQWfTPQoSg@7]. |
| A lone number with no reference | The number plus a time frame, peer, or industry average — never share a single data point in isolation [nuosoMyf0eA@0]. |
| Full paragraphs of prose | Deworded bullet fragments. Dense text will not be read [YXQWfTPQoSg@9], even when printed, and it is way too long for people to understand [3bVc_-m_fMg@11] while listening to you. |
| An entire quote or block in bold | A light grey box behind it. Overuse of bolded text [WZuDhxS40qo@7] destroys the contrast that makes bold work; instead just put a simple box behind them [WZuDhxS40qo@8]. |
| Five font sizes in one body | One body size throughout — everything except the title and footnotes at the same size [YXQWfTPQoSg@2]. |
| Decorative colour | Colour with a job. Every colour needs a direct meaning or a legend [YXQWfTPQoSg@3]; the BCG deck's French-flag arrows were a very unnecessary use of colours [nAlR78kKSTs@10]. |
| A second highlight shade for "average" | Plain grey. A lighter green beside a darker green reads busy — a more decent grey [nAlR78kKSTs@9] keeps the highlight legible. |
| Animations and builds | A static page. Animation is not the look and feel for C-level presentations [YXQWfTPQoSg@12], partly because these decks are often printed out as documents rather than presented [YXQWfTPQoSg@12]. |
| A chart screenshotted out of a report | A chart rebuilt natively from the underlying numbers — never just copy-paste charts like that [YXQWfTPQoSg@8]. |
| An unlabelled percentage | Percentage of what — units, dollars, users. Always add what the percentage is of [YXQWfTPQoSg@6]. |
| A legend occupying its own column | A legend at the bottom; it could have moved down [9WCPpEIIdrE@5]. But do include one — always double-check the legend is there [nAlR78kKSTs@5]. |
| One row off the grid | Everything on the same grid. A stray row reads as an error [9WCPpEIIdrE@7], and across pages, jittering elements [YXQWfTPQoSg@3] are a quiet tell. Final pass: make sure there is no jarring on your pages [YXQWfTPQoSg@4]. |
| Indistinguishable gradient steps | Shades far enough apart to read as different values — more difference between the colour shades [9WCPpEIIdrE@8]. |
| Seven footnotes | Two. Footnotes are the elegant place for sources, but don't overdo footnotes [nAlR78kKSTs@6]. |
| An insider chart form | A form your specific room can read. Nobody understands the football-field chart outside of banking [YXQWfTPQoSg@13]. |

Two additions rather than deletions, both cheap: thin separator lines between grouped elements [YXQWfTPQoSg@5], whose absence is often the only thing making a decent page feel unfinished; and connector lines [9WCPpEIIdrE@7] that make the link between a label and its data point explicit instead of inferred.

## The room: preparation, objections, limits

Delivery in this corpus is not about charisma. The difference between clear and muddled is the amount of preparation someone put into the words [Ck-LeYlAVbY@26] — hence the mentor's line, fail to prepare, prepare to fail [Ck-LeYlAVbY@27]. And you cannot acquire it passively; you can't be an effective presenter by just watching videos [Ck-LeYlAVbY@23].

### Ask, don't pitch

- The fastest way to win a room is to let it do most of the talking [eyGUsY1f2a8@7] and feed you the ideas you then respond to.
- Aim for the shift where the audience narrates their own problem back and you probe — them doing a lot of the storytelling [hahPTeXJEBk@7].
- Overpreparing facts is a trap: presenters who do it retreat into that comfort zone [eyGUsY1f2a8@13] under pressure, exactly when a mixed room needs them not to.
- Success is mutual: someone who takes control of the situation [eyGUsY1f2a8@10] so the audience got value and you learned what you needed.

### Give every segment a hook

- Build the narrative like a Disney film — content for the technical people and separate themes the business people relate to [eyGUsY1f2a8@14] — so nobody switches off.
- Explain complexity in plain terms, using fewer words and less jargon [eyGUsY1f2a8@16].
- Blend the registers until the audience can't see the join [eyGUsY1f2a8@16] between the technical and the business conversation.
- Always connect method to need: the top skill is explaining your technical approach and reasoning [hahPTeXJEBk@20] against business requirements, and deep questions probe why the technology exists [hahPTeXJEBk@16], not tool trivia.

### Handle the "no"

- An objection is an opportunity [eyGUsY1f2a8@19] — dig for the fear or perceived risk behind it rather than defending.
- Reframe rather than hide a limitation: if you can't fix it, feature it [eyGUsY1f2a8@20].
- Read pushback as a start, not a failure: when you hear the no is when the negotiation starts [eyGUsY1f2a8@21].
- Respect emotional ownership of what you're replacing — we sell technology but we sell change [eyGUsY1f2a8@21].
- Learn to spot objections planted by competitors [eyGUsY1f2a8@20], and treat them as intelligence.
- Take the lifeline. When you're off track the room will often throw a steering question [eyGUsY1f2a8@11] — use it instead of digging in.

### Find the real problem first

- What they report is a symptom: the problem is rarely ever the problem [Ck-LeYlAVbY@9].
- The worked case: a "dashboard latency" complaint whose actual goal was sales and conversion, not latency [Ck-LeYlAVbY@10].
- Never flatly contradict the framing — "I would never tell you you're wrong" [Ck-LeYlAVbY@10] — validate it while steering to the underlying goal.
- Quantify the pain at multiple levels [Z9S9oxvYUvk@6] — time on manual work, costly mistakes, competitive risk — and build urgency by asking what happens if you don't do this [Z9S9oxvYUvk@6].
- Borrow credibility where you can: we've done this before, we can do it again [Z9S9oxvYUvk@4].

### Know your limits out loud

- Have the sentence ready: "this is not an area I know about, let me get back to you." [hahPTeXJEBk@6]
- Draw the architecture even when nobody asked, purely to force clarity in your own mind [hahPTeXJEBk@18] — and show it, because the more they understand, the more favourably they judge you [hahPTeXJEBk@19].
- Anchor on one specialisation you can go deep on — think about a particular spike [hahPTeXJEBk@3] rather than claiming breadth everywhere.
- Calibrate by asking: ask your peers about your communication [Ck-LeYlAVbY@28], and practise by writing decisions down so someone with zero context can follow the reasoning [Ck-LeYlAVbY@29].
- Watch the delivery habits you can't hear yourself doing — even strong speakers flag their arms and their "you knows" [Z9S9oxvYUvk@11] on review.

### Plan the window

- A discovery or readout slot is short and fixed — roughly 35 to 40 minutes [eyGUsY1f2a8@8] — so plan its use rather than winging it.
- You are rarely solo; a technical presenter typically works with an account executive [eyGUsY1f2a8@12], so agree who steers what.
- When you need backup, be specific: be good at explaining exactly what help you need [eyGUsY1f2a8@26].
- Carrying yourself in front of senior leadership has a name and it is assessed — executive presence [Ck-LeYlAVbY@22].

One calibration on technical depth: in a pre-sales or mixed-stakeholder setting, the soft-skill layer is the differentiator, and fewer than half [eyGUsY1f2a8@9] of technically excellent people can do it. Technical depth is necessary; the persistent myth is that it is sufficient [eyGUsY1f2a8@22].

## When the deck is an app or a dashboard

No talk in this corpus is about dashboard design — that is the largest gap in the evidence, and it is stated plainly in Gaps. What the corpus does cover, directly and repeatedly, is the two things that decide whether a screen communicates: whether each view states a conclusion, and whether the numbers on it can be traced. The transfers below are marked for what they are.

### Every view needs an action title

- A dashboard page with a chart and no takeaway is the topic-header failure in another medium: descriptive with no point of view [9WCPpEIIdrE@4]. Write the header as the finding, with the number in it — "sales down 12%" [3bVc_-m_fMg@14].
- One goal per screen, exactly as one goal per slide: know the main goal [3bVc_-m_fMg@12] and cut every tile that doesn't serve it.
- Drill-downs are your appendix. Put the focal metric up front and push the nitty-gritty tables behind it [3bVc_-m_fMg@13].
- A direct warning about screens you send rather than narrate: a minimal, visual-only page needs descriptive text under each headline [nAlR78kKSTs@12] if the readers weren't in the room.

### Traceability is the trust mechanism

- Show provenance beside the output: citations or links to the original source documents [uaiq1HvQKoI@21].
- Being able to show which documents produced an answer [uaiq1HvQKoI@17] is what makes an AI-derived insight verifiable rather than a matter of faith.
- Structure synthesised findings as a report highlighting the flagged sections with explanations and citations back to source [uaiq1HvQKoI@31].
- Give the reviewer a risk read so they know how hard to look — an explicit risk assessment tells me how much I need to actually look into it [Ukju3maxbEQ@31]. Attach evidence, not assertions.

### Report quality, not uptime

- Ongoing reporting should carry relevance and hallucination rates [uaiq1HvQKoI@37] alongside cost and alerting — not just whether the service was up.
- A metric doesn't have to be a number: a text or label that helps you understand something [kIvjiH8yJoU@16] counts, and a judge's free-text reasoning is often the most useful column.
- Keep a human path into the raw rows. Eyeballing individual results [kIvjiH8yJoU@11] caught a model gaming retrieval with quoted exact-phrase queries [kIvjiH8yJoU@18] that the scored dashboard did not surface.
- Don't trust aggregate scores alone: single-answer LLM judges are too optimistic [kIvjiH8yJoU@6], and quality monitoring should combine automated evaluation with human review [uaiq1HvQKoI@41].
- Budget realistically for the human half: handing 50 to 100 queries to an expert [kIvjiH8yJoU@5] typically returns one or two before they say they're too busy.
- Build the feedback loop into the surface — thumbs up, thumbs down, comments [uaiq1HvQKoI@41].

### Demo the thing, cheaply, early

- Render the real candidates instead of describing them. Generating a page of options so we can choose the best one [Ukju3maxbEQ@7] converts a debate into a decision.
- A clickable prototype you can deploy and share with your organisation [r-MPiu0E4W4@54] gets direction agreed before you spend on fidelity.
- Reconnect it to the real design system before executives see it, or the verdict is "this is very off-brand" [r-MPiu0E4W4@64].
- Set the expectation gap explicitly. Proofs of concept showcase the wow factor [uaiq1HvQKoI@38]; production meets complexity, limits and cost the demo audience never saw.
- Ship the smallest version that wins: the smallest scope that produces a net increase [Ck-LeYlAVbY@13] — and the advice is bluntly to build a rough first version and iterate [Ck-LeYlAVbY@13].
- Chasing the symptom forever is a trap: latency is a bit of a swamp [Ck-LeYlAVbY@11], where solving it once just raises the next ask.

### Documentation and written updates

- For anything read rather than presented, consistency is the priority [r-MPiu0E4W4@68] — the same structure every time.
- Get consistency from a saved spec, not from prompting afresh: define the types of information that must be included [r-MPiu0E4W4@70] and reuse it.
- Structure written status the same way: recap first, plain language, one question, end with the next action [Ukju3maxbEQ@18].
- You still have to own the reasoning. Asked whether AI can build a design system end to end, the answer is no, it cannot [r-MPiu0E4W4@92].

### Claiming impact in writing

- Use the formula for every claim about your own work: I accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z [5gLVxMKeSGM@18] — result, metric, method.
- Never assume the reader infers significance. If you don't tell them why what you did mattered [5gLVxMKeSGM@2], they won't work it out, and they decide whether to keep reading in five to seven seconds [5gLVxMKeSGM@2].
- When exact figures are confidential, use percentages or multipliers [5gLVxMKeSGM@15], or an order of magnitude [5gLVxMKeSGM@16] like "millions of rows" — never omit scale.
- Write the summary last, after the evidence exists [5gLVxMKeSGM@4], and treat it as a thesis the rest must prove [5gLVxMKeSGM@4].
- Tailor to the stated requirements — like taking an open-book quiz [5gLVxMKeSGM@3] — and let repetition tell you what to lead with [5gLVxMKeSGM@12].
- Check the fit mechanically before you send: paste the document and the audience's requirements into a model and compare the two [5gLVxMKeSGM@21]. In the worked case, iterating moved the score from about 63% to 83% [5gLVxMKeSGM@24].

## Copy these

Four artefacts. The first two are yours to fill in before you build; the last two are review instruments you can paste into a pull request, a design review, or a model.

### Pre-readout brief — fill this in before opening any tool

```
GOAL      (pick one) alignment / education / problem-solving
          By the end they must THINK ______, FEEL ______, DO ______.

AUDIENCE  Decision-maker (who signs off): ______
          Others in the room + what each one fears / wants: ______
          Context level: assume ZERO. Opening recap = 2 sentences: ______

APPROACH  (pick one)
          PYRAMID — answer first, then 3 MECE reasons, then evidence.
          SCR     — situation (agreed facts) / complication (the tension)
                    / resolution (a structured 3-part response).
          Use SCR when the material is data-heavy or the room is sceptical.

FRAMING   Rewrite the recommendation so it does not accuse anyone:
          not "X isn't working" but "directing resources to the
          highest-impact opportunities".
```

Built from the goal/audience/approach structure and the three goal buckets — alignment, education, and problem [c7_riQh50UE@4] — plus the collaborative reframe in the same talk.

### Storyline skeleton — dot-dash, in a document, before any slide

```
THREE-SENTENCE TEST (write this first, revise it last)
  Situation:    ______
  Complication: ______
  Resolution:   ______

DOT-DASH
  • Dot 1 — [full-sentence claim, the future action title]
      – [data point + its comparison]  → becomes chart
      – [data point + its comparison]  → becomes chart
  • Dot 2 — [full-sentence claim]
      – ...
  • Dot 3 — [full-sentence claim]
      – ...
  • Close   — implication + next steps + owner

TWO CHECKS BEFORE YOU OPEN POWERPOINT
  Horizontal: read the dots only. Do they tell the whole argument?
  Vertical:   is anything in a dot unjustified by a dash beneath it?
```

Dot-dash and the two flow checks come from the McKinsey storylining walkthrough, where the dots are the action titles and the dashes turn into charts [CY1Y367KEko@9]; the three-sentence compression is its stated sanity check [CY1Y367KEko@4].

### Review checklist — paste into the PR or the review doc

```
MESSAGE      [ ] Title is a full sentence with the number in it
             [ ] Reading titles alone gives the whole argument
STRUCTURE    [ ] Answer appears in the first minute / first screen
             [ ] Exactly 3 supporting reasons, MECE
             [ ] Long deck opens with an executive summary
EVIDENCE     [ ] Every claim sits on visible data
             [ ] Source note present: how did we get these numbers?
             [ ] Scope defined (which market? which users? which period?)
COMPARISON   [ ] No number stands alone
             [ ] Comparison is like-for-like (not new vs. existing)
PAGE         [ ] One goal per page; everything else cut or appendixed
             [ ] One body font size; hierarchy by size, not boxes
             [ ] One accent colour + black/grey; every colour has meaning
             [ ] Chart type matches the comparison being made
             [ ] All rows on the grid; no jitter page to page
AUDIENCE     [ ] Opens with context; assumes zero
             [ ] No jargon the least technical person can't parse
             [ ] Each stakeholder's fear addressed before it's raised
ACTION       [ ] Closes with implication + next step + owner
             [ ] Impact stated in business terms, not method terms
HONESTY      [ ] Limits, risks and unknowns stated by you
             [ ] Nothing asserted you cannot defend under questioning

SCORE  __/16   (0 absent · 1 partial · 2 strong; below 12 = send back)
```

The scale is this guide's; every line item traces to a cited claim in the rubric above. The "no jitter" line is the final polish pass — make sure that you have no jarring on your pages [YXQWfTPQoSg@4].

### Model prompt — adversarial review of a deck, screen or report

```
You are reviewing work I am about to present to [AUDIENCE, ROLE].
My goal in the meeting is [alignment | education | problem-solving].

Here is the material: [PASTE TEXT / ATTACH SCREENSHOTS]

Do four passes and report each separately.

1. TITLES ONLY. List every title/header in order and nothing else.
   Read that list back to me. Does it tell a complete argument?
   Where does it break, and which title is descriptive rather than a claim?

2. NAKED NUMBERS. List every figure with no comparison point, every
   percentage with no "of what", and every claim with no source note.

3. AUDIENCE. Given the role above, name the three things this person
   will worry about that the material does not address. Name every
   piece of jargon they will not parse.

4. SCORE. Score 0-2 on: message, structure, evidence, comparison,
   page, audience fit, action, honesty. Total out of 16.
   For any dimension under 2, give the single edit that raises it.

Be blunt. Do not compliment the work.
```

The pattern — check your artefact against the audience's stated requirements before sending — is the corpus's, applied to review instead of résumés: compare the document to what the audience asked for [5gLVxMKeSGM@21]. Pair it with a human read; automated judgement alone runs too optimistic [kIvjiH8yJoU@6].

## Where the corpus disagrees

Four live disagreements. Each is a real judgement call, not a defect in the evidence — and the resolution depends on your room.

**Lead with the answer, or build to it?** The pyramid principle says the answer goes first, always — start with the answer first [c7_riQh50UE@11]. The same talk then says that for data-heavy presentations or a sceptical audience you should use situation, complication, resolution [c7_riQh50UE@16] and hold the recommendation until it feels earned. *Call:* pyramid by default; SCR when the audience must be moved off a position they already hold. Pairing them is explicitly endorsed as making a message logically bulletproof and emotionally engaging [c7_riQh50UE@19].

**Finish before you show, or show rough and iterate?** The storylining discipline says settle everything in the document, because later changes mean rebuilding slides [CY1Y367KEko@8]. The forward-deployed engineer says over-polishing before showing work loses deals, so build a rough first version and iterate [Ck-LeYlAVbY@13]. *Call:* they're about different objects. Finalise the *argument* before you build; ship the *artefact* rough and let the customer shape it.

**How many colours can a data page carry?** One review argues a six-colour McKinsey chart actually lowers effort, because matching hues let a viewer group series instinctively and cognitive load is reduced compared to black and white [nuosoMyf0eA@6]. Another, on a BCG page, wanted a second green replaced with a more decent grey [nAlR78kKSTs@9]. *Call:* colours that encode a grouping earn their place; colours that merely decorate do not. Highlight colours should stay decent, not aggressive [nAlR78kKSTs@7].

**How technical should you get?** In a technical assessment, interviewers probe deeper as seniority rises, rewarding depth on why the technologies exist [hahPTeXJEBk@16]. In the situational interview at the same company, leaning on technical depth with a mixed audience is marked down; the job there is discovery, and you should let them do most of the talking [eyGUsY1f2a8@7]. *Call:* depth is what you prove when asked; it is not what you lead with. Note the parallel from consulting — a page banking readers expect can be one that would never go into a consulting main deck [YXQWfTPQoSg@13].

## What this corpus does not answer

Honest gaps. Where you need these, go somewhere else — do not treat the guide above as covering them.

- **Dashboard and app design.** No talk here covers laying out a dashboard, designing a report screen, or choosing charts for an interactive surface. Everything in "When the deck is an app or a dashboard" is slide and document advice transferred by argument, not by evidence.
- **Live delivery mechanics.** Nothing on pacing, vocal tone, body language, or how to open and close a live presentation. The closest the corpus gets is one presenter noticing his own filler words on replay.
- **Remote and asynchronous presenting.** No screen-share etiquette, no camera framing, no guidance on a recorded walkthrough or a written memo replacing a live readout.
- **Hostile Q&A over a chart.** Objection handling here is framed around sales conversations. There is no worked example of defending a forecast or a contested number under live pushback.
- **Statistical rigour.** Nothing on sample size, significance, or confidence — so nothing on deciding when a difference is a real insight rather than noise, and nothing on presenting uncertainty to non-technical stakeholders.
- **Accessibility.** Every source leans on colour coding as a core technique; none mention colourblind-safe palettes or any other accessibility consideration.
- **Deck length and slide budgets.** No numbers on how many slides a 30-minute meeting should carry, how many words are too many for a page, or how long a dewording pass should take.
- **Proof that any of this works.** No win rates, time-to-approval, or lift metrics comparing a structured deck against an unstructured one. The case for SCR and the pyramid principle here is practitioner authority, not measurement.
- **Pre-meeting iteration.** Plenty on inviting input during the meeting, nothing on how to gather and fold in stakeholder feedback on a draft before it.
- **Did it change anything?** No source addresses tracking whether a presentation actually moved a decision or a behaviour afterwards — the one measurement that would close the loop on everything above.
